Tutorial 4 - A few words about quantum computing with superconducting qubits

☕ Loading...

We have seen in the last tutorial the possibilities of quantum computation with trapped ions. However, a second major platform are superconducting qubits. They the platform of choice of commercial giants like google, IBM or Rigetti. In this tutorial, we will identify the existence of qubits in superconducting circuits, the different gates and the read-out. We will finish by a comparison in the computing performance of trapped ions and superconducting qubits.

The quantum LC-oscillator

As in the second tutorial, we have to find the appropiate harmonic oscillator, but this time in electric circuits. Then we can discuss the need of the Josephson junction for the implementation of superconducting qubits.

The fundamental ingredient for superconducting qubits are LC oscillators, which are simply put a loop of wire which is not closed. To study its quantum behavior we will closely follow the discussion in Sec. II of "A Quantum Engineer's Guide to Superconducting Qubits".

In electrical engineering we first have to identify the conjugate variables within the circuit. We will therefore follow the standard procedure of:

  1. Identifying the equations of motion.

  2. Identify the Lagrangien.

  3. Identify the conjugate variables.

  4. Write down the Hamiltonian.

  5. Quantize the Hamiltonian.

While it might be overly complicated for simple LC circuits it provides a powerful framework for more complex systems (see Nigg et al. )

Lagrangien formulation

The wire is caracterized by an inductivity, which is counteracting the change in current:

V=LdIdt\begin{aligned} V = L\frac{dI}{dt}\end{aligned}V=LdtdI​​

and a capacitance, which allows us to measure the cost of putting charges on the ends of the wire:

I=CdVdt\begin{aligned} I = C\frac{dV}{dt}\end{aligned}I=CdtdV​​

To put it under a partical form for quantization we typically express them through the flux, which is defined as:

Φ(t)=∫−∞tV(t′)dt′\begin{aligned} \Phi(t) = \int_{-\infty}^tV(t')dt'\end{aligned}Φ(t)=∫−∞t​V(t′)dt′​

The electromagnetic energy stored within the loop of wire is in general given by:

E(t)=∫−∞tV(t′)I(t′)dt′\begin{aligned} E(t) &= \int_{-\infty}^t V(t')I(t')dt'\end{aligned}E(t)​=∫−∞t​V(t′)I(t′)dt′​

We then obtain the energies:

Ekin=12CΦ˙2Epot=12LΦ2\begin{aligned} E_{kin} = \frac{1}{2}C\dot{\Phi}^2\\ E_{pot} = \frac{1}{2L}\Phi^2\\\end{aligned}Ekin​=21​CΦ˙2Epot​=2L1​Φ2​

This now leads to the Lagrangien:

L=12CΦ˙2−12LΦ2\begin{aligned} L &= \frac{1}{2}C\dot{\Phi}^2-\frac{1}{2L}\Phi^2\end{aligned}L​=21​CΦ˙2−2L1​Φ2​

Quantization

We can now identify the conjugate momentum the flux as:

∂L∂Φ˙=CΦ˙=Q\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\Phi}} &= C \dot{\Phi}\\ &= Q\end{aligned}∂Φ˙∂L​​=CΦ˙=Q​

So the charge is the conjugate variable to the flux in the loop. They will be therefore the two fundamental variables of quantum theory, very much like position and momentum are for massive particles.

We can now write down the Hamiltonian as:

H=QΦ˙−L=Q22C+Φ22L\begin{aligned} H &= Q\dot{\Phi}- L\\ &= \frac{Q^2}{2C}+\frac{\Phi^2}{2L}\end{aligned}H​=QΦ˙−L=2CQ2​+2LΦ2​​

At this stage we can quantize the system through the commutation relation:

[Φ^,Q^]=iℏH^=Q^22C+Φ^22L\begin{aligned} [\hat{\Phi},\hat{Q}]&= i\hbar\\ \hat{H} &= \frac{\hat{Q}^2}{2C}+\frac{\hat{\Phi}^2}{2L}\end{aligned}[Φ^,Q^​]H^​=iℏ=2CQ^​2​+2LΦ^2​​

So it is once again a harmonic oscillator with resonance frequency ωr=1LC\omega_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}ωr​=LC​1​ and 'mass' CCC. So the system reads:

H^=ℏωr(a^†a+12)\begin{aligned} \hat{H} &= \hbar \omega_r \left(\hat{a}^\dagger a + \frac{1}{2}\right)\end{aligned}H^​=ℏωr​(a^†a+21​)​

While this is now a quantum system, it is manifestly not a qubit as the transitions are equidistant in energy with ωr\omega_rωr​. The typical order of magnitude is here 3-6GHz.

To prepare for the introduction of superconducting elements, we typically rewrite the equations above in terms of dimensionless quantities. Namely the Cooper pair density n=Q2en = \frac{Q}{2e}n=2eQ​ and the reduced flux ϕ=2πΦ/Φ0\phi= 2\pi \Phi/\Phi_0ϕ=2πΦ/Φ0​ with Φ0=h2e\Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e}Φ0​=2eh​. These two quantities correspond directly to the density and the phase of the superconducting wavefunction that we will discuss in the next section. We then obtain the Hamiltonian H^=4ECn2+12ELφ2\begin{aligned} \hat{H} = 4E_C n^2 + \frac{1}{2}E_L \varphi^2\end{aligned}H^=4EC​n2+21​EL​φ2​ So we quantify the influence of each lump element through their energy:

  • EC=e22CE_C=\frac{e^2}{2C}EC​=2Ce2​ is the energy required to add a cooper pair.

  • EL=(Φ0/2π)2LE_L=\frac{(\Phi_0/2\pi)^2}{L}EL​=L(Φ0​/2π)2​ is the inductive energy

The Josephson junction

To resolve the degeneracy we need to make the oscillator anharmonic. This is done through Josephson junctions, which are the backbone of superconducting electronics (very much like the transistor or the diode are classical electronics). To understand them roughly, we will fall back on the Feynman picture of Josephson dynamics.

A simplistic picture of superconductivity

We could spend several lectures to understand the physics of Josephson junctions in all its gory details. A good overview might be found in the following books (D.R. Tilley, 1990; Tinkham, 2004). However, the basic idea is that the fermionic electrons form cooper pairs at very low temperatures. These pairs are bosonic and can hence condense into a macroscopic wavefunction:

ψ(x,t)=neiφ(x,t)\begin{aligned} \psi(x,t) &= \sqrt{n}e^{i\varphi(x,t)} \end{aligned}ψ(x,t)​=n​eiφ(x,t)​

Now the system can be understood through the following relations:

  • the density is given by n=∣ψ(x,t)∣2n= |\psi(x,t)|^2n=∣ψ(x,t)∣2.

  • The velocity is set by the gradient of the phase v⃗=ℏ2me∇φ\vec{v}= \frac{\hbar}{2m_e}\nabla \varphiv=2me​ℏ​∇φ.

  • The voltage is set by the time evolution of the phase V=ℏ2e∂φ∂tV = \frac{\hbar}{2e} \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial t}V=2eℏ​∂t∂φ​.

The Josephson relations

A Josephson junction describes now a system where two superconducting regions are slightly separated by a normal metal such that only tunneling is allowed between the two regions.

We can now write down the Schrödinger equation for this setup:

iℏ∂tψL=eV2ψL+JψRiℏ∂tψR=−eV2ψR+JψL\begin{aligned} i\hbar \partial_t \psi_L &= \frac{eV}{2}\psi_L+J \psi_R\\ i\hbar \partial_t \psi_R &= -\frac{eV}{2}\psi_R+J \psi_L\end{aligned}iℏ∂t​ψL​iℏ∂t​ψR​​=2eV​ψL​+JψR​=−2eV​ψR​+JψL​​

VVV is the voltage applied to the junction and JJJ is the tunneling element. We now use the decomposition to write:

n˙L=2ℏJnRnLsin⁡(δ)n˙R=−2ℏJnRnLsin⁡(δ)ϕ=φL−φR\begin{aligned} \dot{n}_L &= \frac{2}{\hbar}J\sqrt{n_Rn_L}\sin(\delta )\\ \dot{n}_R &= -\frac{2}{\hbar}J\sqrt{n_Rn_L}\sin(\delta)\\ \phi &=\varphi_L-\varphi_R\end{aligned}n˙L​n˙R​ϕ​=ℏ2​JnR​nL​​sin(δ)=−ℏ2​JnR​nL​​sin(δ)=φL​−φR​​

We can now use it to write down the current-phase relationship:

I=Icsin⁡(ϕ)Ic=2ℏJn\begin{aligned} I &= I_c\sin(\phi)\\ I_c &= \frac{2}{\hbar}Jn\end{aligned}IIc​​=Ic​sin(ϕ)=ℏ2​Jn​

We can once again integrate the equation of motion to obtain:

H^=4ECn2−EJcos⁡(ϕ)EC=e22(C+CJ)EJ=ICΦ02π\begin{aligned} \hat{H} = 4E_Cn^2-E_J \cos(\phi)\\ E_C = \frac{e^2}{2(C+C_J)}\\ E_J = \frac{I_C\Phi_0}{2\pi}\end{aligned}H^=4EC​n2−EJ​cos(ϕ)EC​=2(C+CJ​)e2​EJ​=2πIC​Φ0​​​

Single qubit control

Superconducting qubits can be controlled either through inductive or capacitive coupling. Inductive coupling is widely used for flux-qubits like the rf-squid. However, here we focus on the transmon qubit, which is typically capacitavely coupled

Going through the quantization procedure we discussed above, we can write the circuit Hamiltonian as:

H^=Q~22CΣ+Φ22L+CdCΣVd(t)Q~\begin{aligned} \hat{H} &= \frac{\tilde{Q}^2}{2C_\Sigma}+\frac{\Phi^2}{2L}+\frac{C_d}{C_\Sigma}V_d(t)\tilde{Q}\end{aligned}H^​=2CΣ​Q~​2​+2LΦ2​+CΣ​Cd​​Vd​(t)Q~​​

The charge is defined for this system as Q~=CΣΦ˙−CdVd(t)\tilde{Q} = C_\Sigma\dot{\Phi} - C_d V_d(t)Q~​=CΣ​Φ˙−Cd​Vd​(t). In the limit of weak coupling CdVd≪CΣΦ˙C_d V_d \ll C_\Sigma \dot{\Phi}Cd​Vd​≪CΣ​Φ˙, we have can quantize the system as before and only need to understand the influence of the last term.

The second part of the Hamiltonian resembles strong the electric dipole coupling we discussed in the last lecture. It contains the displaced charge, which is linearly coupled to an oscillating electric field. So we can rewrite the charge once again in terms of raising an lower operators and arrive at the coupled Hamiltonian:

H^=ωt2σ^z+ΩVd(t)σ^y\begin{aligned} \hat{H} &= \frac{\omega_t}{2}\hat{\sigma}_z+\Omega V_d(t)\hat{\sigma}_y\end{aligned}H^​=2ωt​​σ^z​+ΩVd​(t)σ^y​​

All the other discussions are equivalent to our discussion on the ion and any other single qubit system.

Generating entanglement

Having identified the qubit, we can now also implement the entanglement gate to build the universal quantum computer. Different options exist:

  • The qubit island could be coupled through a mutual capacitance, such that H^int=CgV1V2\hat{H}_{int}= C_g V_1 V_2H^int​=Cg​V1​V2​.

  • The qubit island could be coupled through a mututal inductance, such that H^int=M12I1I2\hat{H}_{int}= M_{12} I_1 I_2H^int​=M12​I1​I2​.

Typically the inductive coupling is chosen in a regime of very small coupling Cg≪C1,C2C_g \ll C_1, C_2Cg​≪C1​,C2​, where the CiC_iCi​ describe the transmon qubits. The full Hamiltonian reads then:

H^=H^T,1+H^T,2+4e2CgC1C2n1n2\begin{aligned} \hat{H} &=\hat{H}_{T,1}+\hat{H}_{T,2}+4e^2\frac{C_g}{C_1C_2}n_1n_2\end{aligned}H^​=H^T,1​+H^T,2​+4e2C1​C2​Cg​​n1​n2​​

we identified here Vi=2eCiniV_i = \frac{2e}{C_i}n_iVi​=Ci​2e​ni​. We can now further rewrite the occupation in terms of raising and lowering operators n∝i(a−a†)n\propto i(a-a^\dagger)n∝i(a−a†), which is can be expressed as a Pauli matrix for the buttom manifold. So we actually have the coupling:

H^=H^T,1+H^T,2+gσy,1σy,2\begin{aligned} \hat{H} &=\hat{H}_{T,1}+\hat{H}_{T,2}+g\sigma_{y,1}\sigma_{y,2}\end{aligned}H^​=H^T,1​+H^T,2​+gσy,1​σy,2​​

While this basic operating principle of capacitive coupling is indeed widely used, it is worth to read the fine-print as the different actual implementation can to different 2-Qubit gates:

  • The iSWAP gate is the implementation of the σyσy\sigma_y \sigma_yσy​σy​ coupling.

  • The phase gate, shows very high fidelities, but makes it necessary to tune the freuqency of the qubit. It implements a σzσz\sigma_z \sigma_zσz​σz​-coupling on the spins. Fidelities of > 99% were demonstrated for this gate (Barends et al., 2014).

  • The cross-resonance (CR) gate is only controlled through microwaves. It implements the a σxσz\sigma_x \sigma_zσx​σz​-coupling on the spins. This is gate employed by IBM (Chow et al., 2011).

A CNOT gate constructed from the physical entangling gates

We would now like to discuss how we can use the capacitive coupling to implement a CNOT gates. This discussion is closely related to the possibility of using a Soerensen-Molmer gate, discussed in lecture 2, to implement a CNOT gate.

The σyσy\sigma_y \sigma_yσy​σy​ coupling for the right amount of leads to a coupling matrix:

iSWAP=(100000−i00−i000001)\begin{aligned} iSWAP &= \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 &-i & 0\\ 0 & -i & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right)\end{aligned}iSWAP​=​1000​00−i0​0−i00​0001​​​

The iSWAP can then be used to represent the CNOT gate.

So we will focus shortly on the current limitations of qubit systems. Please be aware that this is a rapidly evolving field, so most likely the paragraph will be outdated within a few months. One good summary can be found in (Linke et al., 2017) and written by an ion trapping group.

Summmary

In this tutorial, we discussed how the two level system is implemented in superconducting devices and how they are coupled. This should give you a basic feeling for this widely used hardware platform and its limitations.
