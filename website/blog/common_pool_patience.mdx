## The commons, and its two benchmarks

We now formalize the fishery problem, so that we can work out quantitatively what patience is worth.
I use the fishery from the [earlier post](/blog/14/), which is the textbook one — John Leach sets
it up in §8.1 of _A Course in Public Economics_.
Island 1 sends out $b_1$ boats and island 2
sends $b_2$ — that is the whole decision either of them makes. The fleet on the water in season
$t$ is $B_t = b_1 + b_2$ and the fish stock is $s_t$. Three equations close the system:

- **Catch.** $y_t = q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t}$ — more boats land more fish, but with diminishing returns,
  and a richer stock is easier to fish. The catchability $q$ says how good the gear is.
- **Renewal.** $g(s) = r\,s\,(1 - s/K)$ — the usual logistic regrowth, with intrinsic rate $r$
  and carrying capacity $K$. Fastest at half of $K$, zero at both ends.
- **What is left.** $s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - y_t$ — this season's catch sets next season's
  starting point.

The catch is shared in proportion to boats and each boat costs $c$ to send, so a season's profit
for island $j$ is

$$
u_j(b_1, b_2) = b_j\,(v - c), \qquad v \equiv \frac{y}{B} = \frac{q\,s}{\sqrt{B}},
$$

where $v$ is what one boat-trip is worth — it falls as the ground gets crowded, which is the only
reason any of this is interesting. Writing $u_{j,t}$ for what that comes to in season $t$,
what island $j$ actually wants to maximise is its **discounted payoff** over all seasons,

$$
U_j = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\,u_{j,t}.
$$

A geometric weight in time, exactly like a damping factor. In economics $\beta$ tells us how much
next year counts compared to this one, so $\beta$ near 1 is a patient
islander and $\beta = 0$ is one who does not think about next season at all. This parameter will matter
enormously in the following.

One decoration recurs below: a subscript $t$ means "in season $t$". The symbol $t$ is always the
season, never anything else.

There are two natural benchmarks, and we can derive both directly.

**Independent fisheries.** Boats keep going out while there is anything left to gain, so entry
stops where a boat's catch only just covers what it costs to send: $v = c$, or $y/B = c$, or

$$
B_{\text{open}} = \left(\frac{q\,s_0}{c}\right)^2 .
$$

With an initial stock $s_0 = 100$, $q = 0.01$ and $c = 0.125$ that is **64 boats**, earning exactly
nothing between them — the entire value of the catch is spent on the boats that caught it. And
notice what the equation does not contain: the growth rate. Nobody deciding whether to sail is
thinking about next year's stock at all, so the ground is fished down and stays depleted.

**The planner.** The other answer is to hand the fishery to one manager who chooses the fleet for
every season at once, maximising the two islands' discounted profits together. Their profits add
to $B_t(v_t - c)$, so the objective is discounted catch minus discounted cost:

$$
\max_{\{B_t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}\; U = U_1 + U_2 = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\Big(q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t} - c\,B_t\Big)
$$

$$
\text{subject to}\qquad s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t}, \qquad t = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

with $s_0$ given. We will not solve this in general, but perturbatively in $\beta \ll 1$. We will further write down the
boats as a polynomial in $\beta$.

$$
B_t = B_t^{(0)}+\beta B_t^{(1)}+\cdots
$$

And we also have

$$
U= U^{(0)}+ \beta U^{(1)}+\cdots
$$

We can now plug in and find:

$$
U =  \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\Big(q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t} - c\,B_t\Big)
$$

$$
U =  \left(q s_0\sqrt{B_0} - c B_0\right)+\beta\left(q s_1\sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right)
$$

We now need to develop $\sqrt{B} = \sqrt{B^{(0)}+\beta B^{(1)}} \approx \sqrt{B^{(0)}} (1+\frac{B^{(1)}}{B^{(0)}}\beta)$.
Let us plug in:

$$
U =  \left(q\,s_0\sqrt{B_0} - c\,B_0\right)+\beta\left(q s_1\sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right)
$$

It is really easy for zeroth order in $\beta$. We get:

$$
U^{(0)} =  \left(q\,s_0\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0} - c\,B^{(0)}_0\right)
$$

And we try to optimize these profits:

$$
\frac{\partial U^{(0)}}{\partial B_0^{(0)}} =  \frac{q\,s_0}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}} - c
$$

This gives us:

$$
B_0^{(0)} =\left(\frac{q s_0}{2 c}\right)^2 \\
U^{(0)} = \frac{q^2 s_0^2}{4c}
$$

|                       | boats on the water | profit |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | ------ |
| independent fisheries | 64                 | 0      |
| the planner           | 16                 | 2.00   |

Four times the fleet, for nothing at all. That gap is the tragedy of the commons, and it is the
distance any institution has to close.

### First order

Now let us go to first order in $\beta$:

$$
U = q s_0\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}\left(1+\frac{\beta B_0^{(1)}}{2B_0^{(0)}}\right) - c B_0^{(0)} -c \beta B_0^{(1)}+\beta\left(q s_1\sqrt{B^{(0)}_1} - c B^{(0)}_1\right)\\
U = U^{(0)} + \beta\left(s_0\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}\left(\frac{B_0^{(1)}}{2B_0^{(0)}}\right) -c B_0^{(1)}+ \left(q s_1\sqrt{B^{(0)}_1} - c B^{(0)}_1\right)\right)
$$

This gets us:

$$
U^{(1)} = \frac{s_0 B_0^{(1)}}{2\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}} -c (B_0^{(1)}+ B^{(0)}_1)+ q s_1\sqrt{B^{(0)}_1} \\
U^{(1)} = B_0^{(1)}\left[\frac{q s_0}{2\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}} - c\right] + \left(q s_1\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}} - cB_1^{(0)}\right)
$$

This first part cancels as it is just the derivative of $U^{0}$ in the expansion and we get:

$$
U^{(1)} = q s_1\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}} - cB_1^{(0)}
$$

Optimizing for $B_1^{(0)}$ gives us:

$$
B_1^{(0)} =\left(\frac{q s_1}{2 c}\right)^2 \\
U^{(1)} = \frac{q^2 s_1^2}{4c}
$$

We now get to first order the result:

$$
U = \frac{q^2}{4c}\left( s_0^2 + \beta s_1^2\right)
$$

And here we now have the stock in the next season, which starts to be important. Let us plug in:

$$
s_1 = s_0 (1+r-q \sqrt{B^{(0)}_0})\\
s_1 = s_0 (1+r-\frac{q^2 s_0}{2c})
$$

### Second order

This is the one, where it gets technical as a number of terms show up:

$$
B_t = B_t^{(0)}+\beta B_t^{(1)}+\beta^2 B_t^{(2)}+\cdots
$$

We then have:

$$
U =  \left(q s_0 \sqrt{B_0} - c B_0\right)+\beta \left(q s_1 \sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right)+\beta^2 \left(q s_2 \sqrt{B_2} - c B_2\right)
$$

We now need to develop the first term to second order in $\beta$

$$
\sqrt{B} = \sqrt{B^{(0)}+\beta B^{(1)}+\beta^2 B^{(2)}} \approx \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \frac{\beta B^{(1)}+\beta^2 B^{(2)}}{2B^{(0)}}- \frac{ \beta^2 B_{(1)}^2}{8 B_{(0)}^2}\right)\\
\sqrt{B} \approx \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}}{2B^{(0)}}+ \frac{\beta^2}{2B^{(0)}} \left[B^{(2)}- \frac{ B_{(1)}^2}{4 B^{(0)}}\right]\right)
$$

And this also means:

$$
s_{1} = s_0 + rs_0 - qs_0 \sqrt{B^{(0)}_0} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}_0}{2B^{(0)}_0}\right)\\
s_{1} = s_1^{(0)} + \beta s_1^{(1)}\\
s_1^{(1)} = -  \frac{ qs_0}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}}B^{(1)}_0
$$

Let us go to second order:

$$
\left(q s_0 \sqrt{B_0} - c B_0\right) = q s_0 \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}}{2B^{(0)}}+ \frac{\beta^2}{2B^{(0)}} \left[B^{(2)}- \frac{ B_{(1)}^2}{4 B^{(0)}}\right]\right) - c \left(B^{(0)}_0+\beta B^{(1)}_0+\beta^2 B^{(2)}_0\right) \\
= U^{(0)}- \beta^2 \frac{q s_0}{8\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}} \frac{ B_{(1)}^2}{ B^{(0)}}\\
= U^{(0)}- \beta^2 c^3 \frac{ B_{(1),0}^2}{ q^2 s_0^2 }\\
$$

On the second term we get:

$$
\left(q s_1 \sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right) = q s_1 \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}}{2B^{(0)}}\right) - c (B^{(0)}_1+\beta B^{(1)}_1)\\
= U^{(1)}
$$

$$
q s_1 \sqrt{B_1} - c B_1 = q\left(s_1^{(0)} + \beta s_1^{(1)}\right)\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}}\left(1 + \beta \frac{B_1^{(1)}}{2B_1^{(0)}}\right) - c\left(B_1^{(0)} + \beta B_1^{(1)}\right) \\
= \underbrace{\left(q s_1^{(0)}\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}} - c B_1^{(0)}\right)}_{U^{(1)}} + \beta \underbrace{B_1^{(1)}\left[\frac{q s_1^{(0)}}{2\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}}} - c\right]}_{\text{cancels}} + \beta \underbrace{q s_1^{(1)} \sqrt{B_1^{(0)}}}_{\text{survives}} + O(\beta^2)
$$

And the third term gives directly:

$$
\left(q s_2 \sqrt{B_2} - c B_2\right) = \left(q s_2 \sqrt{B^{(0)}_2} - c B^{(0)}_2\right)
$$

Putting it all together we have:

$$
U^{(2)} =\underbrace{- c^3 \frac{ B_{(1),0}^2}{ q^2 s_0^2 }}_{t=0}+ \underbrace{-\frac{q^2 s^{(0)}_1}{2} B^{(1)}_0}_{t=1}+ \underbrace{\left(q s_2 \sqrt{B^{(0)}_2} - c B^{(0)}_2\right)}_{t=2}
$$

We can use this to take the two derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial U^{(2)}}{\partial B_{(1),0}} = - 2 c^3 \frac{ B_{(1),0}}{ q^2 s_0^2 }-\frac{q^2 s^{(0)}_1}{2}\\
\frac{\partial U^{(2)}}{\partial B^{(0)}_{2}} = \frac{q s_2}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_2}}  - c
$$

Now let us set to zero to find:

$$
B^{(0)}_{2} = \left(\frac{qs_2}{2c}\right)^2\\
B^{(1)}_0  = - \frac{q^4 s_0^2 s^{(0)}_1}{4c^3}
$$

That second result is the one we were after, and it says something simple once the clutter is
divided out. Measured against the impatient fleet $B^{(0)}_0 = (q s_0 / 2c)^2$, the correction is

$$
\frac{B^{(1)}_0}{B^{(0)}_0} = -\frac{q^2 s^{(0)}_1}{c} = -2\,V_0'\big(s^{(0)}_1\big),
\qquad V_0'(s) \equiv \frac{q^2 s}{2c},
$$

where $V_0'$ is the shadow price of the stock — what one more fish left in the water is worth next
season. Patience does not change the planner's rule; it puts a price tag on the catch. Every fish
landed today is a fish missing tomorrow, so a boat-trip is worth not $v$ but $v(1 - \beta V_0')$,
and that one substitution is the whole first-order result. It is also the only door the growth
rate ever comes through: $r$ sits inside $s^{(0)}_1$ and nowhere else, which is why the impatient
fleet contains no $r$ at all.

Why _twice_ the price, though? Because the catch goes as $\sqrt{B}$. Boats crowd each other, so
the fleet scales as the square of what a trip is worth: shave one percent off the value of the
catch and you shave two percent off the fleet. Writing $\pi \equiv \beta V_0'(s^{(0)}_1)$ for the
dimensionless price of a fish still swimming, everything above resums into the form worth
remembering,

$$
B_0(\beta) = B^{(0)}_0\,\big(1 - \pi\big)^2 ,
\qquad \pi = \beta\,\frac{q^2 s^{(0)}_1}{2c} ,
$$

which expands back to the coefficient above. A physicist can read $\pi$ as a coupling and the
myopic answer as the free theory: patience renormalises the cost of a boat, $c \to c/(1-\pi)$, and
the planner then runs the same one-season sum they always did. Here $\pi = 0.0395\,\beta$, so the
fleet comes off its impatient value by about eight percent per unit of patience.

### The sustainable regime

We can now analyse how much patience would be needed to move
towards a sustainable regime. First note, that the growth rate is not explicitly in the
correction of the boats. The patient fisher cares about the total stock tomorrow, but not
really about its growth rate explicitly. This is a different question. We can use now the condition:

$$
q \sqrt{B_0} < r
$$

We can now use the usual expension to have:

$$
q \sqrt{B_0^{(0)}} \left(1 + \frac{\beta}{2} \frac{B_0^{(1)}}{B_0^{(0)}}\right) < r\\
q^2 \frac{s_0}{2c} \left(1 - \frac{\beta}{2} \frac{q^2 s^{(0)}_1}{c}\right) < r\\
$$

## Appendix: how the dynamic numbers were computed

Beyond small $\beta$ the expansion above is no use, and the sequence has to be given up on
altogether in favour of a rule. Nothing carries between seasons except the stock, so the best
fleet can depend only on today's stock — not on the date, not on history. Writing $V(s)$ for the
best discounted profit obtainable from today on, the whole infinite problem folds into one
equation,

$$
V(s) = \max_{B}\Big[\,q\,s\sqrt{B} - c\,B \;+\; \beta\,V\big(s + g(s) - q\,s\sqrt{B}\big)\Big]
$$

— this season's profit plus the discounted value of the stock left behind. Differentiating in $B$,
and writing $s'$ for next season's stock, the planner's fleet satisfies

$$
\frac{v}{2}\Big(1 - \beta\,V'(s')\Big) = c .
$$

This is the rule derived above, with the exact shadow price $V'(s')$ in place of the first-order $V_0'(s')$, and
it is implicit: $V'$ depends on the path, which depends on the policy, which depends on $V'$.
Hence the numerics.

The planner is the same calculation with one agent choosing the total fleet.
