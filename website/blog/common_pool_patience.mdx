## The commons, and its two benchmarks

We now formalize the fishery problem, so that we can work out quantitatively what patience is worth.
I use the fishery from the [earlier post](/blog/14/), which is the textbook one — John Leach sets
it up in §8.1 of _A Course in Public Economics_.
Island 1 sends out $b_1$ boats and island 2
sends $b_2$ — that is the whole decision either of them makes. The fleet on the water in season
$t$ is $B_t = b_1 + b_2$ and the fish stock is $s_t$. Three equations close the system:

- **Catch.** $y_t = q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t}$ — more boats land more fish, but with diminishing returns,
  and a richer stock is easier to fish. The catchability $q$ says how good the gear is.
- **Renewal.** $g(s) = r\,s\,(1 - s/K)$ — the usual logistic regrowth, with intrinsic rate $r$
  and carrying capacity $K$. Fastest at half of $K$, zero at both ends.
- **What is left.** $s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - y_t$ — this season's catch sets next season's
  starting point.

The catch is shared in proportion to boats and each boat costs $c$ to send, so a season's profit
for island $j$ is

$$
u_j(b_1, b_2) = b_j\,(v - c), \qquad v \equiv \frac{y}{B} = \frac{q\,s}{\sqrt{B}},
$$

where $v$ is what one boat-trip is worth — it falls as the ground gets crowded, which is the only
reason any of this is interesting. Writing $u_{j,t}$ for what that comes to in season $t$,
what island $j$ actually wants to maximise is its **discounted payoff** over all seasons,

$$
U_j = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\,u_{j,t}.
$$

A geometric weight in time, exactly like a damping factor. In economics $\beta$ tells us how much
next year counts compared to this one, so $\beta$ near 1 is a patient
islander and $\beta = 0$ is one who does not think about next season at all. This parameter will matter
enormously in the following.

There are two natural benchmarks, and we can derive both directly.

**Independent fisheries.** Boats keep going out while there is anything left to gain, so entry
stops where a boat's catch only just covers what it costs to send: $v = c$, or $y/B = c$, or

$$
B_{\text{open}} = \left(\frac{q\,s_0}{c}\right)^2 .
$$

With an initial stock $s_0 = 100$, $q = 0.01$ and $c = 0.125$ that is **64 boats**, earning exactly
nothing between them — the entire value of the catch is spent on the boats that caught it. And
notice what the equation does not contain: the growth rate. Nobody deciding whether to sail is
thinking about next year's stock at all, so the ground is fished down and stays depleted.

**The planner.** The other answer is to hand the fishery to one manager who chooses the fleet for
every season at once, maximising the two islands' discounted profits together. Their profits add
to $B_t(v_t - c)$, so the objective is discounted catch minus discounted cost:

$$
\max_{\{B_t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}\; U = U_1 + U_2 = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\Big(q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t} - c\,B_t\Big)
$$

$$
\text{subject to}\qquad s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t}, \qquad t = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

with $s_0$ given. It will turn out to be **16 boats** earning **2.00** between them, against the 64
of open access:

|                       | boats on the water | profit |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | ------ |
| independent fisheries | 64                 | 0      |
| the planner           | 16                 | 2.00   |

Four times the fleet, for nothing at all. That gap is the tragedy of the commons, and it is the
distance any institution has to close.

Getting the 16 is the rest of this post, and two simplifications make it much easier.

The first is that the planner does not care who catches what — only the total counts. So the
island label can go: write $u \equiv u_1 + u_2$ for the whole fleet's profit in a single season.
It is the same $u$ as before with the $j$ dropped, and it means that **from here on a subscript is
always a season.** $B_t$, $s_t$, $y_t$ — nothing but seasons will be written down there again.

The second is that almost nothing in the problem depends on the square root, or on logistic
regrowth, or on fish. So let us not carry them. Keep $u(B,s)$ for one season's profit from a fleet
$B$ on a stock $s$, and write $\sigma(B,s)$ for the stock it leaves behind:

$$
U = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t} u(B_t, s_t), \qquad s_{t+1} = \sigma(B_t, s_t).
$$

This fishery is the case $u(B,s) = q s\sqrt{B} - cB$ and
$\sigma(B,s) = s + g(s) - q s\sqrt{B}$, but we will not need that until the very end.

Both take two arguments, so a derivative has to say which one it acts on. Everything below is
written $\partial_B u$, $\partial_s u$, $\partial_{BB} u$ — never a prime, never a subscript,
since the subscript slot is spoken for by seasons. One more piece of shorthand pays for itself:
$\big(\partial_B u\big)_t$ means that derivative **evaluated in season $t$**, at the fleet and
stock the impatient planner would have there. So the subscript is still a season, exactly as
promised.

These are the quantities economists call **marginal**, and the word is less forbidding than it
looks: it is simply their way of saying _the derivative of_. What it does not say on its own is
_with respect to what_ — that lives in the phrase that follows. The noun names what gets
differentiated and the "of …" names what it is differentiated by, so $\partial_B u$ is the
marginal profit **of a boat** and $\partial_s u$ the marginal profit **of a fish**. The "of" and
the subscript on $\partial$ are carrying exactly the same information; the notation is just the
more honest of the two, since it can never quietly leave the "of" out.

All the derivation asks is that $u$ be concave in $B$ — so $\partial_{BB} u < 0$ — and that the
optimum be interior. Solve perturbatively in $\beta \ll 1$, with both the fleet and the payoff as
polynomials:

$$
B_t = B_t^{(0)}+\beta B_t^{(1)}+\cdots, \qquad U = U^{(0)}+\beta U^{(1)}+\beta^{2} U^{(2)}+\cdots
$$

### Zeroth order

At $\beta = 0$ only $t = 0$ survives and the planner maximises this season alone:

$$
U^{(0)} = u\big(B_0^{(0)}, s_0\big), \qquad \big(\partial_B u\big)_0 = 0 .
$$

The second equation is the **myopic rule** — the best fleet if next year did not exist — and it
defines a policy $B^{(0)}(s)$, one fleet for each stock. That is the entire zeroth order.

Keep the condition $\partial_B u = 0$ in view, though. It is about to do a great deal of work: at
every order from here on, some term arrives multiplied by it and vanishes. A physicist will
recognise the pattern as Hellmann–Feynman — corrections to the optimum are invisible to the
quantity being optimised, at leading order.

### First order

Two things feed the $\beta^{1}$ coefficient: season 1 at zeroth order, and the first-order
correction to season 0's fleet. Expanding $u$ around $B_0^{(0)}$,

$$
U^{(1)} = \underbrace{\big(\partial_B u\big)_0\,B_0^{(1)}}_{=\;0} + u\big(B_1^{(0)}, s_1^{(0)}\big)
$$

and the first term vanishes — its bracket is the zeroth-order condition. **The correction to
today's fleet drops out of the first-order payoff.** Season 0's fleet was already optimal, so
nudging it changes this season's profit only at second order.

What survives is the same one-season problem, one season later. Maximising over $B_1^{(0)}$
returns the myopic rule at the new stock:

$$
B_1^{(0)} = B^{(0)}\big(s_1^{(0)}\big), \qquad s_1^{(0)} = \sigma\big(B_0^{(0)}, s_0\big) .
$$

So through first order

$$
U = u\big(B_0^{(0)}, s_0\big) + \beta\,u\big(B_1^{(0)}, s_1^{(0)}\big) + O(\beta^{2}),
$$

the same expression twice, one season apart. That is what "order $k$ looks $k$ seasons ahead"
means concretely.

But notice what we have not got. $B_0^{(1)}$, the correction to _today's_ fleet and the thing we
actually want, has cancelled. No further work on $U^{(1)}$ will produce it. Quantum mechanics has
the same wall: $E^{(1)} = \langle\psi_0|H'|\psi_0\rangle$ needs only $\psi_0$, and $\psi^{(1)}$
cannot be recovered from it. It appears at second order.

### Second order

Three seasons now contribute, season $t$ entering at boat-order $2-t$. Two of the three collapse
on sight.

**Season 0** contributes its second-order Taylor remainder. The $B_0^{(2)}$ piece arrives
multiplied by $\big(\partial_B u\big)_0 = 0$ and dies exactly as $B_0^{(1)}$ did one order ago,
leaving only the quadratic $\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\partial_{BB} u\big)_0\big(B_0^{(1)}\big)^2$.

**Season 1** carries an explicit $\beta$, so it reaches $\beta^2$ through its own first-order
parts. Its $B_1^{(1)}$ piece is multiplied by $\big(\partial_B u\big)_1$ — zero for the same
reason. What survives is the dependence on the _stock_, because $s_1$ is not a constant: it
inherits a $\beta$-dependence from $B_0$, namely
$s_1^{(1)} = \big(\partial_B \sigma\big)_0\,B_0^{(1)}$.

**Season 2** contributes a fresh myopic season. Altogether

$$
U^{(2)} = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\partial_{BB} u\big)_0\big(B_0^{(1)}\big)^{2}}_{t=0}
\;+\; \underbrace{\big(\partial_s u\big)_1\,\big(\partial_B \sigma\big)_0\,B_0^{(1)}}_{t=1}
\;+\; \underbrace{u\big(B_2^{(0)}, s_2^{(0)}\big)}_{t=2} .
$$

$B_0^{(1)}$ now appears twice, and in two structurally different ways: **quadratically** through
season 0, **linearly** through season 1. That pairing is what makes the problem well posed. A
downward parabola plus a line has one interior maximum — with only the line the correction would
run away, and with only the parabola it would sit at zero and patience would never move anything.
The two terms are the cost of leaving the myopic optimum and the reward for the stock that buys.

Setting $\partial U^{(2)} / \partial B_0^{(1)} = 0$:

$$
B_0^{(1)} = -\frac{\big(\partial_B \sigma\big)_0\;\big(\partial_s u\big)_1}{\big(\partial_{BB} u\big)_0}
$$

That is the whole result, and it is three derivatives of two functions. How hard the control
pushes the state, what a richer state is worth next season, and how sharply this season's profit
falls away from its peak — nothing else about a problem matters at this order. In quantum
mechanics the move is Hylleraas's, the first-order correction to the state fixed by making the
second-order energy stationary, with the curvature $\partial_{BB} u$ sitting exactly where an
energy denominator would.

Note what $\big(\partial_s u\big)_1$ is: the **marginal value of one more fish** — how much extra
profit next season's fleet makes from a slightly fuller sea. It is an exchange rate between fish
and money, and that is precisely the number you need in order to weigh a fish caught today against
a fish left for tomorrow.

Since $\partial_{BB} u < 0$, the sign of the correction is the sign of
$\big(\partial_B \sigma\big)_0\big(\partial_s u\big)_1$: **the control moves
toward whatever raises tomorrow's value.** For a fishery, where effort depletes a stock worth
having, that means fewer boats. For a problem whose control builds a valuable stock instead, the
same formula says to do more of it.

### What this becomes for a harvest problem

Specialise now, but only halfway. Suppose profit is the catch minus a linear cost, and the stock
loses exactly what is caught:

$$
u(B,s) = y(B,s) - c\,B, \qquad \sigma(B,s) = s + g(s) - y(B,s) .
$$

All three ingredients read off directly. The myopic rule $\partial_B u = 0$ becomes
$\partial_B y = c$: **send boats until one more boat's catch only just covers what that boat
costs.** The marginal value of a fish is $\partial_s u = \partial_s y$ — a fish left in the water
is worth the extra catch it enables. And $\partial_B \sigma = -\partial_B y$, which at the myopic
optimum is exactly $-c$: one more boat costs the stock precisely $c$ worth of fish. So

$$
B_0^{(1)} = \frac{c\;\big(\partial_s y\big)_1}{\big(\partial_{BB}\, y\big)_0} .
$$

**The renewal function has vanished.** $g$ appears nowhere in the correction — it enters only by
setting what $s_1^{(0)}$ is. That is worth pausing on, because it is tempting to assume a patient
planner is one who fishes below the growth rate. It is not. The planner is buying _stock_, not
_growth_: a fuller sea is cheaper to fish next season, and that is the entire mechanism. How fast
the fish breed matters only through how full the sea will be.

Patience, then, does not change the planner's rule. It puts a price tag on the catch: every fish
landed today is a fish missing tomorrow, so a boat-trip is worth not $v$ but
$v\big(1-\beta\,\partial_s y\big)$. Writing $\pi \equiv \beta\big(\partial_s y\big)_1$ for that
dimensionless price, the fleet responds at a rate set by the curvature,

$$
\frac{\delta B}{B_0^{(0)}} = \frac{c}{B_0^{(0)}\,\big(\partial_{BB}\, y\big)_0}\;\delta\pi ,
$$

which for a catch going as $B^{\alpha}$ is $-1/(1-\alpha)$. Diminishing returns amplify the price:
the flatter the profit peak, the further the fleet slides for a given nudge. A physicist can read
$\pi$ as a coupling and the myopic answer as the free theory — patience renormalises the cost of a
boat, $c \to c/(1-\pi)$, and the planner then runs the same one-season sum as always.

### The sustainable regime

Sustainability is a separate question from patience, and the derivation above makes clear why.
Fishing sustainably means taking no more than grows back,

$$
y\big(B_0, s_0\big) < g(s_0),
$$

which compares the catch to the renewal — but we have just seen that the planner's correction
never looks at $g$ at all. Nothing in $B_0^{(1)}$ is steering toward this condition; a patient
planner satisfies it only by accident, if the fleet it wants happens to be small enough.

So the honest way to ask how much patience buys sustainability is to substitute the expansion into
the condition and see what $\beta$ it demands. Since $\partial_B y = c$ at the myopic optimum,
$y(B_0,s_0) \simeq y\big(B_0^{(0)},s_0\big) + \beta\,c\,B_0^{(1)}$, and the condition becomes

$$
y\big(B_0^{(0)}, s_0\big) + \beta\,c\,B_0^{(1)} < g(s_0) .
$$

Whether any $\beta \le 1$ satisfies it is a question about the specific numbers, and for this
fishery the answer is instructive — see below.

## The fishery, worked out

Now put the square root back in. Everything above needed three derivatives of the catch function,
so here they are for $y = q\,s\sqrt{B}$, with $q = 0.01$, $c = 0.125$, $r = 0.03$, $K = 1000$ and
$s_0 = 100$.

| ingredient      | general                             | for $y = q s\sqrt{B}$               | value             |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| myopic rule     | $\partial_B y = c$                  | $q s / 2\sqrt{B} = c$               | —                 |
| myopic fleet    | $B^{(0)}(s)$                        | $\left(q s / 2c\right)^{2}$         | **16 boats**      |
| myopic profit   | $y - cB$ at $B^{(0)}$               | $q^{2} s^{2} / 4c$                  | **2.00**          |
| value of a fish | $\partial_s y$                      | $q^{2} s / 2c$                      | 0.0395 at $s_1$   |
| curvature       | $\partial_{BB}\, y$ at $B^{(0)}$    | $-2c^{3} / q^{2} s^{2}$             | $-0.0039$         |
| **correction**  | $c\,\partial_s y / \partial_{BB} y$ | $-q^{4} s_0^{2} s_1^{(0)} / 4c^{3}$ | $\mathbf{-1.263}$ |

The zeroth order is the 16 boats and 2.00 promised at the start. One season of that fleet lands
$y_0 = 4.00$ and leaves $s_1^{(0)} = 98.7$.

The curvature is the interesting entry: $-0.0039$ is a very flat peak. That is why the fleet is so
responsive — a nudge in the price of fish slides it a long way. Concretely, the response
$c / B_0^{(0)}\big(\partial_{BB}\, y\big)_0$ comes out at exactly $-2$, the $\alpha = 1/2$ case, so
the fleet resums into

$$
B_0(\beta) = B_0^{(0)}\big(1-\pi\big)^{2}, \qquad \pi = \beta\,\frac{q^{2}s_1^{(0)}}{2c} = 0.0395\,\beta,
$$

and comes off its impatient value by about eight percent per unit of patience. **The exponent 2
belongs to the square root, not to the theory** — a catch going as $B^{1/3}$ would give $3/2$.

Finally the sustainability condition, which is where the expansion admits its limit. Substituting,
$4.00 - 0.158\,\beta < 2.70$, so it wants

$$
\beta > 8.2 .
$$

There is no such discount factor. Even a perfectly patient planner, $\beta = 1$, still lands 3.84
against a growth of 2.70 and mines the stock. The reading is not that patience cannot save a
fishery: solving the problem exactly, by the method in the appendix, lifts the long-run stock from
69.8 at $\beta = 0$ to around 90 at $\beta = 0.9$. It is that **sustainability is a large-$\beta$
phenomenon, and a small-$\beta$ expansion is constitutionally unable to see it.** Perturbation
theory tells you which way patience pushes, and how hard, but not where the system ends up.

## Appendix: how the dynamic numbers were computed

Beyond small $\beta$ the expansion above is no use, and the sequence has to be given up on
altogether in favour of a rule. Nothing carries between seasons except the stock, so the best
fleet can depend only on today's stock — not on the date, not on history. Writing $V(s)$ for the
best discounted profit obtainable from today on, the whole infinite problem folds into one
equation,

$$
V(s) = \max_{B}\Big[\,q\,s\sqrt{B} - c\,B \;+\; \beta\,V\big(s + g(s) - q\,s\sqrt{B}\big)\Big]
$$

— this season's profit plus the discounted value of the stock left behind. Differentiating in $B$,
and writing $s'$ for next season's stock, the planner's fleet satisfies

$$
\frac{v}{2}\Big(1 - \beta\,\partial_s V(s')\Big) = c .
$$

This is the rule derived above with one substitution: the exact marginal value of a fish,
$\partial_s V$, stands where the perturbative treatment had the myopic $\partial_s y$. That swap is the whole difference between the
two, and it is what makes the exact problem hard — $\partial_s y$ is a derivative of a function we
were handed, while $\partial_s V$ is a derivative of the answer. It is implicit: $\partial_s V$
depends on the path, which depends on the policy, which depends on $\partial_s V$. Hence the
numerics.

The planner is the same calculation with one agent choosing the total fleet.
