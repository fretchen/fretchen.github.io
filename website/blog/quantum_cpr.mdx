Institutions are a central pillar of economics. However, to me it always felt quite unintuitive how they are constructed.
In this post, I explore how a quantum protocol can generate the necessary institutions to avoid the "tragedy of the commons". This builds upon the previous post in which the I discussed the possibility to solve the
prisoners dilemma through institutions generated by the quantum mechanical extension in the [Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein game](/blog/31/).
The new post extends the complexity of the previous example substantially as it has to work with continuous variables and the dependency
between the different rounds. The successful construction gives a cooking recipe to find institutions for complex economical games.

## The commons and its two benchmarks

A **common-pool resource** is one that many people draw from and nobody can be excluded from: a
fish stock, a groundwater basin, an atmosphere that absorbs emissions. Each user's take reduces
what is left for everyone else, and none of them is charged for it. I will run the whole argument
on the most visual instance, two islands fishing one ground. The general model — and the textbook
fishery John Leach sets up in §8.1 of _A Course in Public Economics_ — is worked through in a
[companion post](/blog/35/). Here is only the notation the rest of this one needs.

Island $j$ sends $b_j$ boats onto the shared ground; the fleet on the water is $B = b_1 + b_2$ and
the stock is $s$. Write $u(B,s)$ for what the whole fishery earns in one season, and give each
island the share matching how hard it worked,

$$
u_j = \frac{b_j}{B}\,u(B,s) .
$$

Over many seasons an island wants its discounted total $U_j = \sum_t \beta^{t} u_{j,t}$, with
$\beta$ near 1 a patient islander and $\beta = 0$ one who does not think about next season at all.

With $N$ users drawing independently, the companion post shows the symmetric equilibrium sitting at

$$
\frac{u}{B} \;=\; \frac{D}{N} , \qquad D \;\equiv\; \frac{u}{B} - \partial_B u .
$$

The damage $D$ corresponds the whole loss your extra boat inflicts on everyone else. The equilibrium condition then reads: you collect the average $u/B$ for your extra boat, but are
charged only $1/N$ of the damage it does. $N \to \infty$ gives $u = 0$ — everyone keeps adding boats until
no profit is left. $N = 1$ gives
$\partial_B u = 0$ — one owner feeling the whole cost of crowding, which is the **planner** in the
economists' sense. The planner reaches a more economic fleet but has plenty of practical issues that come from the centralization
of decision making. You can read more about the problem in this [previous post](/blog/14/).

A quantum protocol gives a third option. A while ago I wrote about the
[quantum prisoner's dilemma](/blog/31/): take the Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein scheme, entangle two
qubits, let each player act on their own qubit, disentangle — and mutual cooperation becomes
stable. Nobody designed an enforcement mechanism. You write down the standard entangling
operation, allow the natural moves, and an institution falls out of the machinery. In the previous
post I exemplified it with a crooked lawyer with a filing cabinet.

## The circuit

The continuous-variable version of EWL is due to
[Li, Du and Massar](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0375-9601(02)01628-6>) — two field modes instead of
two qubits, and instead of a discrete gate each player picks a real number.

Both modes start in vacuum. A referee applies a two-mode squeezer $\hat J(\gamma)$, where the
squeezing parameter $\gamma \ge 0$ is fixed once, in advance. Each island displaces **its own**
mode by the number of boats it wants to send. The referee undoes the squeezing with
$\hat J(\gamma)^\dagger$, and homodyne detection reads out each mode. [Grau-Climent et al.](https://www.mdpi.com/1099-4300/25/12/1585/review_report) showed that what comes out of the
detectors is the **licensed fleet** $b_j^{c}$ that island $j$ is actually credited with:

$$
b_1^{c} = e^{-\gamma}\left(b_1 \cosh\gamma + b_2 \sinh\gamma\right)\\
 b_2^{c} = e^{-\gamma}\left(b_2 \cosh\gamma + b_1 \sinh\gamma\right)
$$

We can analyze the two interesting limits, starting with $\gamma \rightarrow 0$ first. Then we have:

$$
b_{1,2}^{c} \approx b_{1,2} \left(1-\gamma\right) + b_{2,1} \gamma
$$

So we can already see that we really start to care about the number of boats that the other neighbor has sent out. Let us see
what happens in the extreme case $\gamma \rightarrow \infty$. Then we have:

$$
b_{1,2}^{c} \approx \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}
$$

This is exactly the situation of the planner as we care about both boats equally. So we can directly expect that the squeezing leads
to cooperation and solve the tragedy in a similiar fashion as a planner did. However, not by some external institution but by simply sharing
the ressources in a controlled way.

## Solving the tragedy of the commons

We can now look how each individual island would optimize its discounted payoffs for a given squeezing parameter and we get:

$$
u^c_{1,2} = \frac{b^c_{1,2}}{B} u = e^{-\gamma} \left(u_{1,2} \cosh\gamma + u_{2,1} \sinh\gamma\right)
$$

So the payoff is distributed. Note that the licensed fleets sum to the declared total,
$b_1^{c} + b_2^{c} = b_1 + b_2 = B$, so the fleet on the water is unchanged and only its
attribution moves. Write $\alpha = \frac{1+e^{-2\gamma}}{2}$ for the weight each island keeps on
its own declaration, so that $\partial_{b_1} b_1^{c} = \alpha$ and $\partial_{b_1} B = 1$. Each
island now optimizes its own $u_{j}^{c}$ over its declaration $b_j$:

$$
\partial_{b_1} u^c_{1} = (\partial_{b_1} b^c_{1}) \frac{u}{B} + b^c_{1}\,\partial_{B}\!\left(\frac{u}{B}\right)
= \alpha\,\frac{u}{B} - \frac{b^c_{1}}{B}\,D ,
$$

with the damage $D = \frac{u}{B} - \partial_B u$ as in the companion post. Setting this to zero,
and using that the two islands are symmetric so that $b_1^{c} = B/2$:

$$
\frac{u(B,s)}{B} = \frac{D}{2\alpha} = \frac{D}{N_{\text{eff}}}
\qquad \text{with} \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = 2\alpha = 1 + e^{-2\gamma} .
$$

So the squeezing heals the tragedy as it effectively reduces the number of independent islands
through the squeezing parameter. The tragedy disappears at $N_{\text{eff}} = 1$, where only one
island is effectively left and the condition becomes $\partial_B u = 0$, so we optimize total
profits.

## The equivalent classical game

This mechanism is well-known in economics as _cross-ownership_. Each firm holds some equity in other one
and [Reynolds and Snapp](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-7187(86)90027-5>) wrote down
how this solves the tragedy as described above. So we are really back to the situation we had in the Prisoners' dilemma.
The quantum circuit created a mechanism that is well-known in economy and proven to enhance cooperation. It works in both cases,
and in both cases economics had already found the generated institution before.

## Outlook

This finishes the second test of the possibility to generate economic institution through quantum circuits.
In both, writing down a standard entangling operation produced a working
institution, and in both that institution had an entirely classical description —
a filing cabinet, a clerk with a ledger. Here it reaches the planner's outcome knowing only
$\gamma$, and nothing about the resource.

What it cannot change is patience because $\beta$ appears nowhere in
$N_{\text{eff}}$. The protocol removes the externality _between_ the users and leaves the
single-owner problem that remains at whatever patience they had to start with — it reaches the
patient planner if they are patient, and the myopic one if they are not. So in the future,
I am likely to look for further possibilities to use quantum circuits as a cooking recipe for games which require coordination.
Maybe I will come across situations which were solved quite as nicely within economics.
