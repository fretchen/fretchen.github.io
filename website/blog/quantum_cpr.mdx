Institutions are a central pillar of economics. However, to me it always felt quite unintuitive how they are constructed.
In this post, I explore how a quantum protocol can generate the necessary institutions to avoid the "tragedy of the commons"
in the specific example of a fishery. This builds upon the previous post in which the I discussed the possibility to solve the
prisoners dilemma through institutions generated by the quantum mechanical extension in the [Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein game](/blog/31/).
The new post extends the complexity of the previous example substantially as it has to work with continuous variables and the dependency
between the different rounds. The successful construction gives a cooking recipe to find institutions for complex economical games.

## A quick summary of the fishing problem

We study two islands that fish the same ground and want to find the appropiate number of fleets to send out fishing. There are two classical ways to run an arrangement like that, each one with their individual drawbacks.

**Let each island look after itself.** Nobody needs to know anything about the other island and nobody has to agree to
anything. Each island sends the fleet that is best for it given what the other is doing — and because the boats crowd each other, the ground ends up depleted and both islands poorer than they
needed to be. That is the _tragedy of the commons_.

**Or appoint someone to work out the right fleet and impose it** — the planner, in the
economists' sense. This works, and it is the benchmark everything below is measured against. But
that someone has to know how fast the fish
breed, how good the boats are, what a trip costs and how much the islanders care about next year;
they have to redo the sum every time the stock moves; and they have to make the quota stick,
which means watching people and punishing them. The knowledge is scattered across the very people
being planned for, and the enforcement is coercive.

So a good outcome exists. But the choice on offer runs along a tired axis — everyone for
themselves at one end, central planning at the other — and neither end is somewhere you would
want to live. You can read more about the problem in this [previous post](/blog/14/).

I got interested in a third possibility from an odd direction. A while ago I wrote about the
[quantum prisoner's dilemma](/blog/31/): take the Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein scheme, entangle two qubits, let each player act on their own qubit,
disentangle — and mutual cooperation becomes stable. Nobody designed an enforcement mechanism.
You write down the standard entangling operation, allow the natural moves, and an institution
falls out of the machinery. In the previous post I exemplified it with a crooked lawyer with a
filing cabinet.

## The commons, in one page

The general common-pool model — and the textbook fishery I use for numbers, which John Leach sets
up in §8.1 of _A Course in Public Economics_ — is worked through in a
[companion post](/blog/35/). Here is only the notation the rest of this one needs.

Island $j$ sends $b_j$ boats onto a shared ground; the fleet on the water is $B = b_1 + b_2$ and
the stock is $s$. Write $u(B,s)$ for what the whole fishery earns in one season, and give each
island the share matching how hard it worked,

$$
u_j = \frac{b_j}{B}\,u(B,s) .
$$

Over many seasons an island wants its discounted total $U_j = \sum_t \beta^{t} u_{j,t}$, with
$\beta$ near 1 a patient islander and $\beta = 0$ one who does not think about next season at all.

That is enough to state the tragedy. With $N$ islands drawing independently, the companion post
shows the symmetric equilibrium sitting at

$$
\frac{u}{B} \;=\; \frac{D}{N} , \qquad D \;\equiv\; \frac{u}{B} - \partial_B u ,
$$

which reads: you collect the average $u/B$ for your extra boat, but pay only $1/N$ of the damage
$D$ it does. The two ends of that are the benchmarks everything below is measured against.
$N = 1$ gives $\partial_B u = 0$ — one owner, feeling the whole cost of crowding, which is the
planner. $N \to \infty$ gives $u = 0$ — open access, every scrap of rent competed away.

## The circuit, and what it implements

The continuous-variable version of EWL is due to
[Li, Du and Massar](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0375-9601(02)01628-6>) — two field modes instead of
two qubits, and instead of a discrete gate each player picks a real number.

<LDMCircuit />

Both modes start in vacuum. A referee applies a two-mode squeezer $\hat J(\gamma)$, where the
squeezing parameter $\gamma \ge 0$ is fixed once, in advance. Each island displaces **its own**
mode by the number of boats it wants to send. The referee undoes the squeezing with
$\hat J(\gamma)^\dagger$, and homodyne detection reads out each mode. What comes out of the
detectors — their Eq. (11), where the operator algebra is worked through in full — is the
**licensed fleet** $b_j^{c}$ that island $j$ is actually credited with:

$$
b_1^{c} = e^{-\gamma}\left(b_1 \cosh\gamma + b_2 \sinh\gamma\right), \qquad b_2^{c} = b_2 e^{-\gamma}\cosh\gamma + b_1 \sinh\gamma\\
b_{1}^{c} = b_1 \frac{1+e^{-2\gamma}}{2} + b_2 \frac{1-e^{-2\gamma}}{2}\\
b_2^{c} = b_2 \frac{1+e^{-2\gamma}}{2} + b_1 \frac{1-e^{-2\gamma}}{2}
$$

We can analyze the two interesting limits, starting with $\gamma \rightarrow 0$ first. Then we have:

$$
b_{1,2}^{c} \approx b_{1,2} \left(1-\gamma\right) + b_{2,1} \gamma
$$

So we can already see that we really start to care about the number of boats that the other neighbor has sent out. Let us see 
what happens in the extreme case $\gamma \rightarrow \infty$. Then we have:

$$
b_{1,2}^{c} \approx = \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}
$$
This is exactly the situation of the planner as we care about both boats equally. So we can directly expect that the squeezing leads
to cooperation and solve the tragedy in a similiar fashion as a planner did. However, not by some external institution but by simply sharing
the ressources in a controlled way.

### Solving the tragedy of the commons

We can now look how each individual island would optimize its discounted payoffs for a given squeezing parameter and we get:

$$
u^c_{1,2} = \frac{b^c_{1,2}}{B} u(B,s) = e^{-\gamma} \left(u_{1,2} \cosh\gamma + u_{2,1} \sinh\gamma\right)
$$
So the payoff is distributed. Each player now has to optimize the individual $u_{j}^c$ as a function of his decision parameter $b_{j}$, so he looks for:

$$
\partial_{b_j} u^c_{j} = \partial_{b_j}(b^c_{j}) \frac{u}{B}+b^c_{j} \partial_{b_j}\left(\frac{u}{B}\right)
$$

This works out too:
$$
\partial_{b_1} u^c_{1} = e^{-\gamma} \cosh\gamma \frac{u(B,s)}{B}+\frac{b^c_{1}}{B} \left(\partial_{B}u-\frac{u}{B}\right)
$$

So we now find the optimal condition:

$$
\frac{u(B,s)}{B} = \frac{b^c_{1}}{B} D \frac{1}{e^{-\gamma}\cosh{\gamma}}
$$
The prefactor is once again roughly 1/2 (because the two islands are supposed to be equal) and we can now write:
$$
\frac{u(B,s)}{B} = \frac{D}{N_{eff}} \text{ with } N_{eff} = 1+e^{-2\gamma}
$$
So the squeezing heals the tragedy as it effectively reduces the number of independent island through the squeezing parameter. The tragedy disappears for 
$N=1$ as we have effectively only one island left and the optimal condition becomes $\partial_B u = 0$, so we optimize total profits.
Nothing in that used the square root, the logistic curve, or a single parameter value. $u(B,s)$
was arbitrary throughout, so this is a statement about common-pool problems, not about fish.



## The equivalent classical game

The quantum game and a much simpler classical game have the same payoffs —
and therefore the same best replies and the same equilibria. That classical game is: **each
island maximises its own profit plus a share $\lambda$ of the other's**, with $\lambda$ running
from 0 at $\gamma = 0$ to 1 as the squeezing goes up.

Which is a rule a harbour clerk could write on a noticeboard. Your licensed fleet is your own
boats plus a fraction of your neighbour's; you are paid and billed on that number. Nothing in it
needs a laser. This is the continuous-variable version of a point
[van Enk and Pike](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.66.024306) made about EWL: the quantum game
has a fully classical description, and writing it down is not hard.


Deflating as that is for the physics, look at what the clerk is doing, because it is a real
institution and a good one.

Say Moana runs one island and Chief Kai the other. Moana is wondering whether to send one more
boat. Without the rule she weighs the extra catch against the crowding — but only the crowding on
**her own** boats. What it costs Kai is not her problem, and that gap is the whole tragedy.

The clerk closes it by making her a part-owner of Kai's catch. She is paid on a slice of whatever
he lands, so her extra boat now costs her twice: once through the crowding on her own catch, once
through the crowding on the slice of his that she owns. At full squeezing she feels the entire
damage her boat does — and the fleet she then picks, out of nothing but self-interest, is the
16 boats the planner would have imposed. That is the second benchmark, reached without anybody
imposing anything.

There is no agreement between Moana and Kai here. Neither promised anything, nobody is watching,
and there is nothing to enforce. This is not cooperation. Moana is exactly as selfish as before —
the sum in front of her has changed, that is all.

And now compare what the clerk had to know against the model above. Not $q$, not $r$ or $K$, not
$c$, not $\beta$. He never works out how many fish there are, how fast they breed, or what the
right fleet would be. He publishes $\gamma$, applies it to two declared numbers, and the islands
find the 16 themselves. The planner's answer, without the planner's knowledge — and without the
planner.

## What the rule buys

Put numbers on it. For the textbook fishery — catchability $q = 0.01$, a cost of $c = 0.125$ per
boat, stock $s = 100$ — the companion post's $N$-user condition closes to
$B^{*}(N) = (q s/c)^{2}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\big)^{2}$. Substituting $N_{\text{eff}}$ collapses
that to $\sqrt{B} = (3-\lambda)\,q s/4c$: **36 boats** at $\gamma = 0$, falling to **16** as the
squeezing goes up.

<FisheryDial />

Notice where the dial starts. Open access would put 64 boats on the water, but two islands left
alone send only 36, because **two islands are not a crowd** — each already owns half the fleet and
so already feels half the damage it does. Part of the tragedy is solved simply by there being two
of them rather than a hundred. The clerk closes the rest.

The trade is not even painful. Fewer boats means less crowding, and less crowding means each
remaining boat is worth more. The islands end up sending under half the fleet and earning about a
third more.

That dial holds the stock fixed at 100. It shows the mechanism, but a fishery whose stock never
moves is not a fishery. So the real question is whether the clerk still lands on the planner's
answer when the stock rises and falls from one season to the next.

It does, for a fairly boring reason. Adding up discounted seasons is a linear operation. So is
"your own profit plus a share $\lambda$ of the other island's". Two linear operations can be
applied in either order, which is all this says — with $u_{1,t}$ island 1's profit in season $t$,
and $U_1$ its discounted lifetime total, as defined above:

$$
\sum_{t} \beta^{t}\big(u_{1,t} + \lambda\, u_{2,t}\big) \;=\; U_1 + \lambda\, U_2.
$$

Left to right, that is the clerk's rule applied every season and then summed; right to left, it
is each island's lifetime earnings summed first and the clerk's rule applied to those. Same
number. So the share does not stay trapped inside one season — it lands on the lifetime payoffs,
and at full squeezing each island is maximising $U_1 + U_2$, the lifetime earnings of the entire
fishery. Which is precisely the planner's objective above.

Solving the fishery as a proper dynamic game confirms it. Under full squeezing, two selfish
islands settle on the same stock as a planner with the same patience, at every patience level I
tested — 90.1 against 90.1 at $\beta = 0.9$, 375.4 against 376.5 at $\beta = 0.99$. The appendix
says how that was computed.

That leaves $\beta$ doing all the remaining work, and it does a lot of it. Writing the standing
stock as a percentage of what the ground could carry:

| patience $\beta$ | 0.9 | 0.95 | 0.97 | 0.99 |
| ---------------- | --- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| stock reached    | 17% | 24%  | 36%  | 73%  |

An islander who discounts next year by 10% ends up at a sixth of the ceiling — and so does a
planner with that patience, because at that point the binding constraint is no longer the
externality between the islands. It is that both of them, together, would rather have the fish
now.

A word on what "tragedy" does and does not mean here. The fishery never actually dies. Catching
gets expensive as the fish get scarce, which gives them a refuge, so even with no rule at all the
stock settles around 48 rather than going to zero. The commons is not destroyed; it is just
permanently poor, which is the more common way these things go wrong.

Both knobs have a ceiling in practice. Squeezing of $S$ decibels means $e^{2\gamma} = 10^{S/10}$,
so 10 dB — around the state of the art for a stable optical setup — gives $\lambda = 9/11$, about
0.82. Respectable. The patience column is the harder one to buy.

So: $\gamma$ decides how well the islands coordinate, $\beta$ decides how much fish there is to
coordinate over.

## Where this sits

I set this up as a choice between two bad options, which is exactly the framing
[Elinor Ostrom](https://www.actu-environnement.com/media/pdf/ostrom_1990.pdf) spent her career
demolishing. Where
[Hardin](https://math.uchicago.edu/~shmuel/Modeling/Hardin,%20Tragedy%20of%20the%20Commons.pdf)
concluded a commons ends in ruin unless it is privatised or policed, she showed that communities
routinely govern them without either, and distilled eight features shared by the ones that
survived — clear boundaries, the users making their own rules, monitoring each other, graduated
sanctions.

So there is already a third way, and the clerk is not it. A couple of Ostrom's principles are
about what a rule _does_, and the clerk satisfies those. Most are about who makes the rule, who
watches, and who may change it — and there the clerk is closer to the planner than to a
community, since $\gamma$ is imposed from outside and nobody can argue with it. A rule welded
into an optical bench is about as unrevisable as a rule gets, which is backwards from what she
found makes commons work.

Her principles are empirical regularities distilled from case studies, not a mathematical design,
and half of them are not statements about payoffs at all. Working out which of them _could_ be
expressed as a mechanism is an open problem, and an interesting one.

## Outlook

Two data points now. In both, writing down a standard entangling operation produced a working
institution nobody designed, and in both that institution had an entirely classical description —
a filing cabinet, a clerk with a ledger. Here it reaches the planner's outcome on the planner's
own terms, and it does so knowing one number instead of five.

What it cannot reach is time. An entangling operation acts within a round, and a commons is a
problem about rounds. My guess is that the boundary can be moved — by pricing fish left in the
water rather than boats sent out — but only classically, bolted onto the ledger rather than the
circuit.

If you have thought about that, or about which of Ostrom's principles could be given a
mathematical form, I would like to hear from you. My working notes and reading list are
[here](/blog/32/).

## Appendix: how the dynamic numbers were computed

The one-season figures behind the dial are a closed form, and the arithmetic sits in the
[component itself](https://github.com/fretchen/fretchen.github.io/blob/main/website/components/blog/FisheryDial.tsx).
The multi-season ones need more work, so here is what was actually solved.

Solving the planner's problem above in closed form is not possible — the companion post pushes a
perturbative approach as far as it goes — so the sequence has to be given up on altogether in
favour of a rule. Nothing carries between seasons except the stock, so the best
fleet can depend only on today's stock — not on the date, not on history. Writing $V(s)$ for the
best discounted profit obtainable from today on, the whole infinite problem folds into one
equation,

$$
V(s) = \max_{B}\Big[\,q\,s\sqrt{B} - c\,B \;+\; \beta\,V\big(s + g(s) - q\,s\sqrt{B}\big)\Big]
$$

— this season's profit plus the discounted value of the stock left behind. Differentiating in $B$,
and writing $s'$ for next season's stock, the planner's fleet satisfies

$$
\frac{v}{2}\Big(1 - \beta\,V'(s')\Big) = c .
$$

This is the same one-season rule as above, $v/2=c$, with the exact shadow price $V'(s')$ built in, and
it is implicit: $V'$ depends on the path, which depends on the policy, which depends on $V'$.
Hence the numerics.

Each island's fleet is therefore a function of the current stock — a policy, not a number — and
the two policies have to be a mutual best reply. I find them by iterating: fix one island's
policy, value-iterate the other's equation above on a grid of stock levels, take the best reply,
repeat. The planner is the same calculation with one agent choosing the total fleet.

One trap is worth recording, because it cost me a debugging round and it is invisible from the
output. Write $B^{*}$ for the equilibrium total fleet. At full squeezing both islands maximise the
_same_ function of the total, so island 1's best reply to $b_2$ is **any** $b_1$ with
$b_1 + b_2 = B^{*}$. The best-reply slope is exactly $-1$, and undamped iteration cycles with
period two forever while still returning a perfectly plausible-looking policy. The update has to
be damped.

Two checks keep it honest. At $\beta = 0$ the solver reproduces the static Nash closed form and
the long-run stock of 47.6 that follows from it. And for the linear (Cournot) case it reproduces
Li–Du–Massar's own published Eq. (13) and Eq. (14) exactly, at every $\gamma$ tested — which
checks the payoff reduction above, not just the solver. The
[notebook](https://github.com/fretchen/fretchen.github.io/blob/main/notebooks/quantum_cpr_v2.ipynb)
runs both checks as assertions. It writes the four constants as `y0, g0, g1, c0`, which are the
$q$, $r$, $1/K$ and $c$ used here.
