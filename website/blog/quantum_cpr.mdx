Institutions are a central pillar of economics. However, to me it always felt quite unintuitive how they are constructed.
In this post, I explore how a quantum protocol can generate the necessary institutions to avoid the "tragedy of the commons"
in the specific example of a fishery. This builds upon the previous post in which the I discussed the possibility to solve the
prisoners dilemma through institutions generated by the quantum mechanical extension in the [Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein game](/blog/31/).
The new post extends the complexity of the previous example substantially as it has to work with continuous variables and the dependency
between the different rounds. The successful construction gives a cooking recipe to find institutions for complex economical games.

## A quick summary of the fishing problem

We study two islands that fish the same ground and want to find the appropiate number of fleets to send out fishing. There are two classical ways to run an arrangement like that, each one with their individual drawbacks.

**Let each island look after itself.** Nobody needs to know anything about the other island and nobody has to agree to
anything. Each island sends the fleet that is best for it given what the other is doing — and because the boats crowd each other, the ground ends up depleted and both islands poorer than they
needed to be. That is the _tragedy of the commons_.

**Or appoint someone to work out the right fleet and impose it** — the planner, in the
economists' sense. This works, and it is the benchmark everything below is measured against. But
that someone has to know how fast the fish
breed, how good the boats are, what a trip costs and how much the islanders care about next year;
they have to redo the sum every time the stock moves; and they have to make the quota stick,
which means watching people and punishing them. The knowledge is scattered across the very people
being planned for, and the enforcement is coercive.

So a good outcome exists. But the choice on offer runs along a tired axis — everyone for
themselves at one end, central planning at the other — and neither end is somewhere you would
want to live. You can read more about the problem in this [previous post](/blog/14/).

I got interested in a third possibility from an odd direction. A while ago I wrote about the
[quantum prisoner's dilemma](/blog/31/): take the Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein scheme, entangle two qubits, let each player act on their own qubit,
disentangle — and mutual cooperation becomes stable. Nobody designed an enforcement mechanism.
You write down the standard entangling operation, allow the natural moves, and an institution
falls out of the machinery. In the previous post I exemplified it with a crooked lawyer with a
filing cabinet.

## The commons, and its two benchmarks

We now formalize the fishery problem, to roll out the quantum approach quantitatively on it.
I use the fishery from the [earlier post](/blog/14/), which is the textbook one — John Leach sets
it up in §8.1 of _A Course in Public Economics_.
Island 1 sends out $b_1$ boats and island 2
sends $b_2$ — that is the whole decision either of them makes. The fleet on the water in season
$t$ is $B_t = b_1 + b_2$ and the fish stock is $s_t$. Three equations close the system:

- **Catch.** $y_t = q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t}$ — more boats land more fish, but with diminishing returns,
  and a richer stock is easier to fish. The catchability $q$ says how good the gear is.
- **Renewal.** $g(s) = r\,s\,(1 - s/K)$ — the usual logistic regrowth, with intrinsic rate $r$
  and carrying capacity $K$. Fastest at half of $K$, zero at both ends.
- **What is left.** $s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - y_t$ — this season's catch sets next season's
  starting point.

The catch is shared in proportion to boats and each boat costs $c$ to send, so a season's profit
for island $j$ is

$$
u_j(b_1, b_2) = b_j\,(v - c), \qquad v \equiv \frac{y}{B} = \frac{q\,s}{\sqrt{B}},
$$

where $v$ is what one boat-trip is worth — it falls as the ground gets crowded, which is the only
reason any of this is interesting. Writing $u_{j,t}$ for what that comes to in season $t$,
what island $j$ actually wants to maximise is its **discounted payoff** over all seasons,

$$
U_j = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\,u_{j,t}.
$$

A geometric weight in time, exactly like a damping factor. In economics $\beta$ tells us how much
next year counts compared to this one, so $\beta$ near 1 is a patient
islander and $\beta = 0$ is one who does not think about next season at all. This parameter will matter
enormously in the following.

Three decorations recur below: a subscript $t$ means "in season $t$", a superscript $c$ means "as
cleared by the referee", and a tilde means "rescaled". The symbol $t$ is always the season.

We can now derive the two benchmarks from the introduction directly.

**Independent fisheries.** Boats keep going out while there is anything left to gain, so entry
stops where a boat's catch only just covers what it costs to send: $v = c$, or $y/B = c$, or

$$
B_{\text{open}} = \left(\frac{q\,s_0}{c}\right)^2 .
$$

With an initial stock $s_0 = 100$, $q = 0.01$ and $c = 0.125$ that is **64 boats**, earning exactly
nothing between them — the entire value of the catch is spent on the boats that caught it. And
notice what the equation does not contain: the growth rate. Nobody deciding whether to sail is
thinking about next year's stock at all, so the ground is fished down and stays depleted.

**The planner.** The other answer is to hand the fishery to one manager who chooses the fleet for
every season at once, maximising the two islands' discounted profits together. Their profits add
to $B_t(v_t - c)$, so the objective is discounted catch minus discounted cost:

$$
\max_{\{B_t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}}\; U = U_1 + U_2 = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\Big(q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t} - c\,B_t\Big)
$$

$$
\text{subject to}\qquad s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t}, \qquad t = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

with $s_0$ given. We will not solve this in general, but perturbatively in $\beta \ll 1$. We will further write down the
boats as a polynomial in $\beta$.

$$
B_t = B_t^{(0)}+\beta B_t^{(1)}+\cdots
$$

And we also have

$$
U= U^{(0)}+ \beta U^{(1)}+\cdots
$$

We can now plug in and find:

$$
U =  \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^{t}\Big(q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t} - c\,B_t\Big)
$$

$$
U =  \left(q s_0\sqrt{B_0} - c B_0\right)+\beta\left(q s_1\sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right)
$$

We now need to develop $\sqrt{B} = \sqrt{B^{(0)}+\beta B^{(1)}} \approx \sqrt{B^{(0)}} (1+\frac{B^{(1)}}{B^{(0)}}\beta)$.
Let us plug in:

$$
U =  \left(q\,s_0\sqrt{B_0} - c\,B_0\right)+\beta\left(q s_1\sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right)
$$

It is really easy for zeroth order in $\beta$. We get:

$$
U^{(0)} =  \left(q\,s_0\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0} - c\,B^{(0)}_0\right)
$$

And we try to optimize these profits:

$$
\frac{\partial U^{(0)}}{\partial B_0^{(0)}} =  \frac{q\,s_0}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}} - c
$$

This gives us:

$$
B_0^{(0)} =\left(\frac{q s_0}{2 c}\right)^2 \\
U^{(0)} = \frac{q^2 s_0^2}{4c}
$$

### First order

Now let us go to first order in $\beta$:

$$
U = q s_0\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}\left(1+\frac{\beta B_0^{(1)}}{2B_0^{(0)}}\right) - c B_0^{(0)} -c \beta B_0^{(1)}+\beta\left(q s_1\sqrt{B^{(0)}_1} - c B^{(0)}_1\right)\\
U = U^{(0)} + \beta\left(s_0\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}\left(\frac{B_0^{(1)}}{2B_0^{(0)}}\right) -c B_0^{(1)}+ \left(q s_1\sqrt{B^{(0)}_1} - c B^{(0)}_1\right)\right)
$$

This gets us:

$$
U^{(1)} = \frac{s_0 B_0^{(1)}}{2\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}} -c (B_0^{(1)}+ B^{(0)}_1)+ q s_1\sqrt{B^{(0)}_1} \\
U^{(1)} = B_0^{(1)}\left[\frac{q s_0}{2\sqrt{B_0^{(0)}}} - c\right] + \left(q s_1\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}} - cB_1^{(0)}\right)
$$

This first part cancels as it is just the derivative of $U^{0}$ in the expansion and we get:

$$
U^{(1)} = q s_1\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}} - cB_1^{(0)}
$$

Optimizing for $B_1^{(0)}$ gives us:

$$
B_1^{(0)} =\left(\frac{q s_1}{2 c}\right)^2 \\
U^{(1)} = \frac{q^2 s_1^2}{4c}
$$

We now get to first order the result:

$$
U = \frac{q^2}{4c}\left( s_0^2 + \beta s_1^2\right)
$$

And here we now have the stock in the next season, which starts to be important. Let us plug in:

$$
s_1 = s_0 (1+r-q \sqrt{B^{(0)}_0})\\
s_1 = s_0 (1+r-\frac{q^2 s_0}{2c})
$$

### Second order

This is the one, where it gets technical as a number of terms show up:

$$
B_t = B_t^{(0)}+\beta B_t^{(1)}+\beta^2 B_t^{(2)}+\cdots
$$

We then have:

$$
U =  \left(q s_0 \sqrt{B_0} - c B_0\right)+\beta \left(q s_1 \sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right)+\beta^2 \left(q s_2 \sqrt{B_2} - c B_2\right)
$$

We now need to develop the first term to second order in $\beta$

$$
\sqrt{B} = \sqrt{B^{(0)}+\beta B^{(1)}+\beta^2 B^{(2)}} \approx \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \frac{\beta B^{(1)}+\beta^2 B^{(2)}}{2B^{(0)}}- \frac{ \beta^2 B_{(1)}^2}{8 B_{(0)}^2}\right)\\
\sqrt{B} \approx \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}}{2B^{(0)}}+ \frac{\beta^2}{2B^{(0)}} \left[B^{(2)}- \frac{ B_{(1)}^2}{4 B^{(0)}}\right]\right)
$$

Let us go to second order:

$$
\left(q s_0 \sqrt{B_0} - c B_0\right) = q s_0 \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}}{2B^{(0)}}+ \frac{\beta^2}{2B^{(0)}} \left[B^{(2)}- \frac{ B_{(1)}^2}{4 B^{(0)}}\right]\right) - c \left(B^{(0)}_0+\beta B^{(1)}_0+\beta^2 B^{(2)}_0\right) \\
= U^{(0)}+ \beta^2 \left[  \left(\frac{q s_0}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}} \left[B^{(2)}- \frac{ B_{(1)}^2}{4 B^{(0)}}\right]\right) - c B^{(2)}_0\right]
$$

On the second term we get:

$$
\left(q s_1 \sqrt{B_1} - c B_1\right) = q s_1 \sqrt{B^{(0)}} \left(1 + \beta \frac{ B^{(1)}}{2B^{(0)}}\right) - c (B^{(0)}_1+\beta B^{(1)}_1)\\
= U^{(1)}
$$

$$
q s_1 \sqrt{B_1} - c B_1 = q\left(s_1^{(0)} + \beta s_1^{(1)}\right)\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}}\left(1 + \beta \frac{B_1^{(1)}}{2B_1^{(0)}}\right) - c\left(B_1^{(0)} + \beta B_1^{(1)}\right) \\
= \underbrace{\left(q s_1^{(0)}\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}} - c B_1^{(0)}\right)}_{U^{(1)}} + \beta \underbrace{B_1^{(1)}\left[\frac{q s_1^{(0)}}{2\sqrt{B_1^{(0)}}} - c\right]}_{\text{cancels}} + \beta \underbrace{q s_1^{(1)} \sqrt{B_1^{(0)}}}_{\text{survives}} + O(\beta^2)
$$

And the third term gives directly:

$$
\left(q s_2 \sqrt{B_2} - c B_2\right) = \left(q s_2 \sqrt{B^{(0)}_2} - c B^{(0)}_2\right)
$$

Putting it all together we have:

$$
U^{(2)} =\underbrace{\left[  \left(\frac{q s_0}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}} \left[B^{(2)}_0- \frac{ \left(B^{(1)}_0\right)^2}{4 B^{(0)}_0}\right]\right) - c B^{(2)}_0\right]}_{t=0}+ \underbrace{q s_1^{(1)} \sqrt{B^{(0)}_1}}_{t=1}+ \underbrace{\left(q s_2 \sqrt{B^{(0)}_2} - c B^{(0)}_2\right)}_{t=2}
$$

The $t=1$ term is the only place $B^{(1)}_0$ survives, and it enters through the stock it leaves
behind. Differentiating the constraint $s_1 = s_0 + g(s_0) - q s_0 \sqrt{B_0}$ gives

$$
s_1^{(1)} = -\frac{q s_0}{2\sqrt{B^{(0)}_0}}\,B^{(1)}_0
$$

so fewer boats in season 0 mean a richer season 1. This is the return on patience, and it is what
makes $\partial U^{(2)} / \partial B^{(1)}_0 = 0$ a condition with content.

There is one constraint per season, and they are the only thing tying the
seasons together — without them the sum would fall apart into independent one-year problems. The
chain runs

$$
B_t \;\longmapsto\; y_t = q\,s_t\sqrt{B_t} \;\longmapsto\; s_{t+1} = s_t + g(s_t) - y_t \;\longmapsto\; B_{t+1}
$$

so this season's fleet never reaches next season's directly. It reaches it only by moving the
stock in between. This is Leach's problem exactly — his §8.2 writes the same sum with
$(1+r)^{-t}$ in place of $\beta^t$ — and his verdict is worth quoting: _complicated, but well
defined and solvable_.

Rather than solve that in general — the appendix does — it is worth expanding in $\beta$, because
the first two orders already show what patience does.

**No patience at all.** At $\beta = 0$ only the first term of the sum survives and the planner
maximises this season's profit, $q s\sqrt{B} - cB$. Setting the derivative to zero gives
$v/2 = c$, so

$$
B_0(s) = \left(\frac{q\,s}{2c}\right)^2, \qquad V_0(s) = \frac{q^2 s^2}{4c} .
$$

At $s_0 = 100$ that is **16 boats**, earning $V_0 = 2.00$ between them — the number already in the
table, now as a function of the stock.

It is worth asking whether that fleet is sustainable, and it is not. Those 16 boats take
$y_0 = q^2 s^2 / 2c = 4.00$ out of the water, while the ground grows back by only
$g(s) = 2.70$ — a catch half again as large as the growth. So the stock falls, and it keeps
falling until the two finally balance at $q^2 s / 2c = r(1 - s/K)$:

$$
s^{*} = \frac{r}{\dfrac{q^{2}}{2c} + \dfrac{r}{K}} \approx 69.8
$$

by which point the fleet is down to 7.8 boats. An impatient manager runs the ground down by thirty
percent _with both islands doing exactly as they are told_. Coordination alone does not save a
fishery; the manager also has to care about next year.

**A little patience.** Turn $\beta$ up slightly. The planner now cares what next season starts
with, and to first order the worth of next season is still $V_0$. So a fish left in the water has
a price, $V_0'(s) = q^2 s / 2c$, and the fleet picks up a correction linear in $\beta$:

$$
B(s;\beta) = B_0(s) + \beta\,B_1(s) + O(\beta^2), \qquad B_1 = -2\,V_0'(s')\,B_0
$$

with $s' = s + g(s) - y_0$ the stock the impatient policy leaves behind. Writing that out gives
the coefficient in full, and it is worth seeing what it is made of:

$$
\frac{B_1}{B_0} = -\frac{q^2}{c}\Big(\underbrace{s}_{\text{still there}} \;+\; \underbrace{g(s)}_{\text{they bred}} \;-\; \underbrace{y_0}_{\text{you caught}}\Big)
$$

Read the correction: the extra boat still lands $v/2$ of marginal catch, but every fish in that
catch would have been worth $\beta V_0'(s')$ had it been left alone. The fleet shrinks. **Patience
is a price on fish in the water**, and the size of the price is the stock you are handing to next
season — the three terms in the bracket being what is left, what it breeds, and what you took.

And there is the growth rate, back in the problem. It was missing from the open-access condition
entirely — nobody deciding whether to sail cared how fast the fish breed — and here it sits in the
middle term, so a quicker-breeding ground draws a deeper cut. Honesty compels a caveat: at this
fishery's $r = 0.03$ that middle term is only about 3% against the leading $1$. To first order,
most of what a fish left behind is worth is simply that it is still there next season.

All of which holds for $\beta \ll 1$. The patient case is the appendix's problem.

|                       | boats on the water | profit |
| --------------------- | ------------------ | ------ |
| independent fisheries | 64                 | 0      |
| the planner           | 16                 | 2.00   |

Four times the fleet, for nothing at all. That gap is the tragedy of the commons, and it is the
distance any institution has to close.

## The circuit, and what it implements

The continuous-variable version of EWL is due to
[Li, Du and Massar](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0375-9601(02)01628-6>) — two field modes instead of
two qubits, and instead of a discrete gate each player picks a real number.

<LDMCircuit />

Both modes start in vacuum. A referee applies a two-mode squeezer $\hat J(\gamma)$, where the
squeezing parameter $\gamma \ge 0$ is fixed once, in advance. Each island displaces **its own**
mode by the number of boats it wants to send. The referee undoes the squeezing with
$\hat J(\gamma)^\dagger$, and homodyne detection reads out each mode. What comes out of the
detectors — their Eq. (11), where the operator algebra is worked through in full — is the
**licensed fleet** $b_j^{c}$ that island $j$ is actually credited with:

$$
b_1^{c} = b_1 \cosh\gamma + b_2 \sinh\gamma, \qquad b_2^{c} = b_2 \cosh\gamma + b_1 \sinh\gamma .
$$

In words: the number of boats you are credited with is your own declaration mixed with your
neighbour's. That mixed number is what you get paid on, and what you get billed for.

Now pull the common factor out of that measurement and give the leftover share a name,
$\lambda = \tanh\gamma$:

$$
b_1^{c} = \cosh\gamma\,\big(b_1 + \lambda\, b_2\big), \qquad \lambda = \tanh\gamma ,
$$

and the same with the labels swapped. The $\cosh\gamma$ is the same for both islands, so it is a
change of units and cannot affect anyone's decision. Feed the rest into the payoffs. Writing
$\tilde b_j = e^{\gamma}b_j$ for the rescaled fleet, island 1's profit under the protocol — call
it $u_1^{Q}$ — comes out as

$$
u_1^{Q} = e^{-\gamma}\cosh\gamma\,\big[\,u_1(\tilde b) + \lambda\,u_2(\tilde b)\,\big],
$$

where $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the ordinary one-season profits from §1, evaluated at the rescaled
fleets. The prefactor is positive and common to both, and the rescaling is just a relabelling of
the declared fleet. So the quantum game and a much simpler classical game have the same payoffs —
and therefore the same best replies and the same equilibria. That classical game is: **each
island maximises its own profit plus a share $\lambda$ of the other's**, with $\lambda$ running
from 0 at $\gamma = 0$ to 1 as the squeezing goes up.

Which is a rule a harbour clerk could write on a noticeboard. Your licensed fleet is your own
boats plus a fraction of your neighbour's; you are paid and billed on that number. Nothing in it
needs a laser. This is the continuous-variable version of a point
[van Enk and Pike](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.66.024306) made about EWL: the quantum game
has a fully classical description, and writing it down is not hard.

## What the clerk actually does

Deflating as that is for the physics, look at what the clerk is doing, because it is a real
institution and a good one.

Say Moana runs one island and Chief Kai the other. Moana is wondering whether to send one more
boat. Without the rule she weighs the extra catch against the crowding — but only the crowding on
**her own** boats. What it costs Kai is not her problem, and that gap is the whole tragedy.

The clerk closes it by making her a part-owner of Kai's catch. She is paid on a slice of whatever
he lands, so her extra boat now costs her twice: once through the crowding on her own catch, once
through the crowding on the slice of his that she owns. At full squeezing she feels the entire
damage her boat does — and the fleet she then picks, out of nothing but self-interest, is the
16 boats the planner would have imposed. That is the second benchmark, reached without anybody
imposing anything.

There is no agreement between Moana and Kai here. Neither promised anything, nobody is watching,
and there is nothing to enforce. This is not cooperation. Moana is exactly as selfish as before —
the sum in front of her has changed, that is all.

And now compare what the clerk had to know against the list from §1. Not $q$, not $r$ or $K$, not
$c$, not $\beta$. He never works out how many fish there are, how fast they breed, or what the
right fleet would be. He publishes $\gamma$, applies it to two declared numbers, and the islands
find the 16 themselves. The planner's answer, without the planner's knowledge — and without the
planner.

## What the rule buys

One thing to settle before turning it up. §1 bracketed the problem between independent fisheries
and the planner, 64 boats against 16. The clerk works on a narrower gap than that, because **two
islands are not a crowd**. Open access has so many boats that no single owner feels the crowding
it causes; with only two, each one already owns half the fleet and so already feels half the
damage it does. Left alone at $\gamma = 0$ they send 36 boats, not 64 — part of the tragedy is
solved simply by there being two of them rather than a hundred. That is where the dial starts,
and the planner's 16 is where it ends.

<FisheryDial />

The trade is not even painful. Fewer boats means less crowding, and less crowding means each
remaining boat is worth more. The islands end up sending under half the fleet and earning about a
third more.

The curve behind that slider is the same stopping condition, with the clerk's share written in.
An island counts its own half of the crowding plus $\lambda$ of its neighbour's, so it stops at
$\sqrt{B} = (3-\lambda)\,q s/4c$ — 36 boats at $\lambda = 0$, and 16 as $\lambda \to 1$. Push it
to $\lambda = -1$, where an island would have to actively disregard its own crowding, and you
recover the 64 of open access. The protocol reaches $\lambda \in [0,1)$, so it starts from two
selfish islands and ends at the planner.

That dial holds the stock fixed at 100. It shows the mechanism, but a fishery whose stock never
moves is not a fishery. So the real question is whether the clerk still lands on the planner's
answer when the stock rises and falls from one season to the next.

It does, for a fairly boring reason. Adding up discounted seasons is a linear operation. So is
"your own profit plus a share $\lambda$ of the other island's". Two linear operations can be
applied in either order, which is all this says — with $u_{1,t}$ island 1's profit in season $t$,
and $U_1$ its discounted lifetime total from §1:

$$
\sum_{t} \beta^{t}\big(u_{1,t} + \lambda\, u_{2,t}\big) \;=\; U_1 + \lambda\, U_2.
$$

Left to right, that is the clerk's rule applied every season and then summed; right to left, it
is each island's lifetime earnings summed first and the clerk's rule applied to those. Same
number. So the share does not stay trapped inside one season — it lands on the lifetime payoffs,
and at full squeezing each island is maximising $U_1 + U_2$, the lifetime earnings of the entire
fishery. Which is precisely what the planner in §1 was maximising.

Solving the fishery as a proper dynamic game confirms it. Under full squeezing, two selfish
islands settle on the same stock as a planner with the same patience, at every patience level I
tested — 90.1 against 90.1 at $\beta = 0.9$, 375.4 against 376.5 at $\beta = 0.99$. The appendix
says how that was computed.

That leaves $\beta$ doing all the remaining work, and it does a lot of it. Writing the standing
stock as a percentage of what the ground could carry:

| patience $\beta$ | 0.9 | 0.95 | 0.97 | 0.99 |
| ---------------- | --- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| stock reached    | 17% | 24%  | 36%  | 73%  |

An islander who discounts next year by 10% ends up at a sixth of the ceiling — and so does a
planner with that patience, because at that point the binding constraint is no longer the
externality between the islands. It is that both of them, together, would rather have the fish
now.

A word on what "tragedy" does and does not mean here. The fishery never actually dies. Catching
gets expensive as the fish get scarce, which gives them a refuge, so even with no rule at all the
stock settles around 48 rather than going to zero. The commons is not destroyed; it is just
permanently poor, which is the more common way these things go wrong.

Both knobs have a ceiling in practice. Squeezing of $S$ decibels means $e^{2\gamma} = 10^{S/10}$,
so 10 dB — around the state of the art for a stable optical setup — gives $\lambda = 9/11$, about
0.82. Respectable. The patience column is the harder one to buy.

So: $\gamma$ decides how well the islands coordinate, $\beta$ decides how much fish there is to
coordinate over.

## Where this sits

I set this up as a choice between two bad options, which is exactly the framing
[Elinor Ostrom](https://www.actu-environnement.com/media/pdf/ostrom_1990.pdf) spent her career
demolishing. Where
[Hardin](https://math.uchicago.edu/~shmuel/Modeling/Hardin,%20Tragedy%20of%20the%20Commons.pdf)
concluded a commons ends in ruin unless it is privatised or policed, she showed that communities
routinely govern them without either, and distilled eight features shared by the ones that
survived — clear boundaries, the users making their own rules, monitoring each other, graduated
sanctions.

So there is already a third way, and the clerk is not it. A couple of Ostrom's principles are
about what a rule _does_, and the clerk satisfies those. Most are about who makes the rule, who
watches, and who may change it — and there the clerk is closer to the planner than to a
community, since $\gamma$ is imposed from outside and nobody can argue with it. A rule welded
into an optical bench is about as unrevisable as a rule gets, which is backwards from what she
found makes commons work.

Her principles are empirical regularities distilled from case studies, not a mathematical design,
and half of them are not statements about payoffs at all. Working out which of them _could_ be
expressed as a mechanism is an open problem, and an interesting one.

## Outlook

Two data points now. In both, writing down a standard entangling operation produced a working
institution nobody designed, and in both that institution had an entirely classical description —
a filing cabinet, a clerk with a ledger. Here it reaches the planner's outcome on the planner's
own terms, and it does so knowing one number instead of five.

What it cannot reach is time. An entangling operation acts within a round, and a commons is a
problem about rounds. My guess is that the boundary can be moved — by pricing fish left in the
water rather than boats sent out — but only classically, bolted onto the ledger rather than the
circuit.

If you have thought about that, or about which of Ostrom's principles could be given a
mathematical form, I would like to hear from you. My working notes and reading list are
[here](/blog/32/).

## Appendix: how the dynamic numbers were computed

The one-season figures behind the dial are a closed form, and the arithmetic sits in the
[component itself](https://github.com/fretchen/fretchen.github.io/blob/main/website/components/blog/FisheryDial.tsx).
The multi-season ones need more work, so here is what was actually solved.

Beyond small $\beta$ the expansion in §1 is no use, and the sequence has to be given up on
altogether in favour of a rule. Nothing carries between seasons except the stock, so the best
fleet can depend only on today's stock — not on the date, not on history. Writing $V(s)$ for the
best discounted profit obtainable from today on, the whole infinite problem folds into one
equation,

$$
V(s) = \max_{B}\Big[\,q\,s\sqrt{B} - c\,B \;+\; \beta\,V\big(s + g(s) - q\,s\sqrt{B}\big)\Big]
$$

— this season's profit plus the discounted value of the stock left behind. Differentiating in $B$,
and writing $s'$ for next season's stock, the planner's fleet satisfies

$$
\frac{v}{2}\Big(1 - \beta\,V'(s')\Big) = c .
$$

This is §1's rule with the exact shadow price $V'(s')$ in place of the first-order $V_0'(s')$, and
it is implicit: $V'$ depends on the path, which depends on the policy, which depends on $V'$.
Hence the numerics.

Each island's fleet is therefore a function of the current stock — a policy, not a number — and
the two policies have to be a mutual best reply. I find them by iterating: fix one island's
policy, value-iterate the other's equation above on a grid of stock levels, take the best reply,
repeat. The planner is the same calculation with one agent choosing the total fleet.

One trap is worth recording, because it cost me a debugging round and it is invisible from the
output. Write $B^{*}$ for the equilibrium total fleet. At full squeezing both islands maximise the
_same_ function of the total, so island 1's best reply to $b_2$ is **any** $b_1$ with
$b_1 + b_2 = B^{*}$. The best-reply slope is exactly $-1$, and undamped iteration cycles with
period two forever while still returning a perfectly plausible-looking policy. The update has to
be damped.

Two checks keep it honest. At $\beta = 0$ the solver reproduces the static Nash closed form and
the long-run stock of 47.6 that follows from it. And for the linear (Cournot) case it reproduces
Li–Du–Massar's own published Eq. (13) and Eq. (14) exactly, at every $\gamma$ tested — which
checks the reduction in §3, not just the solver. The
[notebook](https://github.com/fretchen/fretchen.github.io/blob/main/notebooks/quantum_cpr_v2.ipynb)
runs both checks as assertions. It writes the four constants as `y0, g0, g1, c0`, which are the
$q$, $r$, $1/K$ and $c$ used here.
