A while ago I wrote about the [quantum prisoner's dilemma](/blog/31/) and something in it that
still surprises me. Take the Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein scheme, entangle two qubits, let each
player act on their own qubit, disentangle — and mutual cooperation becomes stable. Nobody sat
down and designed an enforcement mechanism. You write down the standard entangling operation,
allow the natural moves, and an institution falls out of the machinery. In that post it looked
like a crooked lawyer with a filing cabinet.

That is charming, but we already knew how to fix the prisoner's dilemma. The interesting
question is whether the trick works on a problem where we *don't* already know the answer. So:
the tragedy of the commons.

It does, and the institution that falls out is a harbour clerk with a ledger. It dissolves the
part of the tragedy that happens between the two islands. How much fish is left at the end
depends on something else entirely — how patient the islanders are — and the circuit has no grip
on that at all.

## The commons, and what makes it different

Two islands fish the same ground. Each season each island decides how many boats to send. More
boats means more fish for you, but the ground gets crowded and every trip is worth less — to you
and to your neighbour. Your neighbour's losses are not your problem. That is the tragedy, in one
sentence.

I use the fishery from an [earlier post](/blog/14/). A fleet of $B$ boats on a stock $s$ lands a
catch $y = y_0\,s\sqrt{B}$, shared out in proportion to boats; each boat costs $c_0$ to send; and
what is left of the stock regrows logistically. Each island wants to maximise not this season's
profit but its **discounted payoff** — this season's profit, plus next season's weighted by a
factor $\beta$, plus the one after weighted by $\beta^2$, and so on. That $\beta$ is just how much
next year counts compared to this one. It is a geometric weight in time, exactly like a damping
factor, and it will matter enormously.

Two things make this harder than the prisoner's dilemma.

**The choice is continuous.** Not cooperate or defect, but *how many boats*. In the prisoner's
dilemma there were three options and one of them, "flip both", was clever — it worked because
those moves don't commute, so acting on your own qubit could reach across and undo your
partner's. Here every move is "send this many boats", and all of those commute with each other.
There is no hidden non-commuting option waiting to be discovered. Entanglement can only bend the
answer, not add a new one.

**The stock carries over.** The prisoner's dilemma happens once. A fishery happens every season,
and this season's catch sets next season's starting point.

## The circuit, and what it implements

The continuous-variable version of EWL is due to Li, Du and Massar. Two field modes instead of
two qubits, and instead of a discrete gate each player picks a real number.

<LDMCircuit />

Both modes start in vacuum. A referee applies a two-mode squeezer $\hat J(\gamma)$. Each island
displaces **its own** mode by the number of boats it wants to send. The referee undoes the
squeezing with $\hat J(\gamma)^\dagger$, and homodyne detection reads out each mode. The
parameter $\gamma$ is fixed once, in advance. What comes out of the detectors is

$$
b_1^{c} = b_1 \cosh\gamma + b_2 \sinh\gamma, \qquad b_2^{c} = b_2 \cosh\gamma + b_1 \sinh\gamma .
$$

In words: the number of boats you are credited with is your own declaration mixed with your
neighbour's. That mixed number is what you get paid on, and what you get billed for.

Now pull the common factor out of that measurement:

$$
b_j^{c} = \cosh\gamma\,\big(b_j + \tanh\gamma\; b_{-j}\big).
$$

The $\cosh\gamma$ is the same for both islands, so it is a change of units and cannot affect
anyone's decision. Feed the rest into the payoffs. Writing $t = \tanh\gamma$ and
$\tilde b_j = e^{\gamma}b_j$, island $j$'s profit comes out as

$$
u_j^{Q} = e^{-\gamma}\cosh\gamma\,\big[\,u_j(\tilde b) + t\,u_{-j}(\tilde b)\,\big],
$$

where $u_j$ is the ordinary profit it would make with a fleet $\tilde b_j$. The prefactor is
positive and common to both, and $\tilde b_j$ is just a relabelling of the declared fleet. So the
quantum game and a much simpler classical game have the same payoffs — and therefore the same
best replies and the same equilibria. That classical game is: **each island maximises its own
profit plus a share $\lambda = \tanh\gamma$ of its neighbour's**, the share running from 0 at
$\gamma = 0$ to 1 as the squeezing goes up.

Which is a rule a harbour clerk could write on a noticeboard. Your licensed fleet is your own
boats plus a fraction of your neighbour's; you are paid and billed on that number. Nothing in it
needs a laser. This is the continuous-variable version of a point van Enk and Pike made about
EWL: the quantum game has a fully classical description, and writing it down is not hard.

## What the rule does

Deflating as that is for the physics, look at what the clerk is doing, because it is a real
institution and a good one.

Say Moana runs one island and Chief Kai the other. Moana is wondering whether to send one more
boat. Without the rule she weighs the extra catch against the crowding — but only the crowding on
**her own** boats. What it costs Kai is not her problem, and that gap is the whole tragedy.

The clerk closes it by making her a part-owner of Kai's catch. She is paid on a slice of whatever
he lands, so her extra boat now costs her twice: once through the crowding on her own catch, once
through the crowding on the slice of his that she owns. At full squeezing she feels the entire
damage her boat does, and the fleet she picks out of pure self-interest is the fleet a single
owner of the whole fishery would pick.

There is no agreement between Moana and Kai here. Neither promised anything, nobody is watching,
and there is nothing to enforce. This is not cooperation. Moana is exactly as selfish as before —
the sum in front of her has changed, that is all.

And notice what the clerk never needs to know. He does not work out how many fish there are, how
fast they breed, or what the right fleet would be. He publishes one number, $\gamma$, and the
islands find the rest themselves. Anyone setting a quota instead would need the entire model.

The trade is not even painful. Fewer boats means less crowding, and less crowding means each
remaining boat is worth more. The islands end up sending under half the fleet and earning about a
third more.

## Over many seasons

One season is not the interesting case, though. What happens when the stock carries over?

The clerk's rule turns out to survive the passage to many seasons untouched, because discounting
is linear and so is the rule. The two commute:

$$
\sum_{t} \beta^{t}\Big(u_j(t) + \lambda\, u_{-j}(t)\Big) \;=\; U_j + \lambda\, U_{-j}.
$$

The share does not stay trapped inside one season. It passes straight through the discounting and
lands on the **lifetime** payoffs, so each island ends up maximising its own discounted stream
plus a share $\lambda$ of its neighbour's. At full squeezing that is the total, which is exactly
what a single owner of the fishery would maximise.

[Solving the fishery as a proper dynamic game](https://github.com/fretchen/fretchen.github.io/blob/main/notebooks/quantum_cpr_v2.ipynb)
confirms it: under full squeezing, two selfish islands settle on the same stock as one owner with
the same patience, at every patience level I tested.

That leaves $\beta$ doing all the remaining work, and it does a lot of it. Writing the standing
stock as a percentage of what the ground could carry:

| patience $\beta$ | 0.9 | 0.95 | 0.97 | 0.99 |
| ---------------- | --- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| stock reached    | 17% | 24%  | 36%  | 73%  |

An islander who discounts next year by 10% ends up at a sixth of the ceiling — and so does a
sole owner with that patience, because at that point the binding constraint is no longer the
externality between the islands. It is that both of them, together, would rather have the fish
now.

Both knobs have a ceiling in practice. Squeezing of $S$ decibels means $e^{2\gamma} = 10^{S/10}$,
so 10 dB — around the state of the art for a stable optical setup — gives $\lambda = 9/11$, about
0.82. Respectable. The patience column is the harder one to buy.

So: $\gamma$ decides how well the islands coordinate, $\beta$ decides how much fish there is to
coordinate over.

## A note on Ostrom

The obvious question is whether the clerk is an Ostrom institution. Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel for
showing that communities do govern commons successfully, and she distilled eight features shared
by the ones that survived — clear boundaries, the users making their own rules, monitoring each
other, graduated sanctions, and so on.

The honest answer is: barely. A couple of her principles are about what a rule *does*, and the
clerk satisfies those. Most are about who makes the rule, who watches, and who may change it, and
there the clerk scores badly — $\gamma$ is imposed from outside and nobody can argue with it. A
rule welded into an optical bench is about as unrevisable as a rule gets, which is backwards from
what Ostrom found makes commons work.

I want to be careful here. Ostrom's principles are empirical regularities distilled from case
studies, not a mathematical design, and half of them are not statements about payoffs at all.
Working out which of them *could* be expressed as a mechanism is an open problem, and an
interesting one.

## Outlook

Two data points now. In both, writing down a standard entangling operation produced a working
institution that nobody designed, and in both the institution turned out to have an entirely
classical description — a filing cabinet, a clerk with a ledger. In the second case, where the
answer was not obvious beforehand, it genuinely solves the part of the problem it can reach.

What it cannot reach is time. An entangling operation acts within a round, and a commons is a
problem about rounds. My guess is that this boundary can be moved — by pricing fish left in the
water rather than boats sent out — but only classically, bolted onto the ledger rather than the
circuit.

If you have thought about that, or about which of Ostrom's principles could be given a
mathematical form, I would like to hear from you. My working notes and reading list are
[here](/blog/32/).
